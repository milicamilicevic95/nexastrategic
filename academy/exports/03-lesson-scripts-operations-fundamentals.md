## Module 1: What operations is, and why it is commercial

### 1.1 Operations is how the business keeps its promises

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

A working definition of operations, and the four things it holds together: promises, work, people and money.

**Estimated length**

About 6 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: title card "Operations is how the business keeps its promises"]

Most founders I work with use the word "operations" without ever having been told what it means. That's not a criticism. It's a word that gets stretched to cover admin, logistics, HR, finance, IT and "anything that isn't sales". When a word means everything, it stops helping you decide anything.

So here's a working definition for this course.

[ON SCREEN: "Operations is how the business keeps its promises, reliably and at a cost it can afford."]

Operations is how the business keeps its promises, reliably, and at a cost it can afford.

Three parts to that.

**Promises.** Every business makes them. "Next-day delivery." "We'll reply within a working day." "Your website will be live by the end of March." Some promises are written in contracts. Many are made on calls, in proposals, on your website or just by habit. Customers remember all of them.

**Reliably.** Not once, when you're watching. Every time, including the weeks you're on holiday, ill or busy with something else. That's the part this programme is really about.

**At a cost it can afford.** A promise you can only keep by working until midnight, or by paying a freelancer more than the customer pays you, isn't really being kept. It's being subsidised.

[ON SCREEN: four boxes — Promises, Work, People, Money]

To keep promises reliably, operations has to hold four things together.

**The promises** themselves: what you've said you'll deliver, by when, and to what standard.

**The work**: the steps that turn a request into a finished result. Who does what, in what order, and what gets handed from one person to another.

**The people**: who does the work, what they're able to do, how much time they have, and who decides when something isn't straightforward. Suppliers and contractors count here too.

**The money**: what the work costs, what you charge, and when cash comes in and goes out.

When founders say "our operations are a mess", one of these four is usually out of line with the others. Promises have grown faster than the people. The work has changed but the price hasn't. The money arrives three months after the cost.

[ON SCREEN: three businesses side by side]

Let me show you how differently this looks across businesses.

A wholesale bakery promises fresh bread by 7am. Its operations are baking schedules, driver routes and flour deliveries.

A software company promises the product will be available and that support will reply within four hours. Its operations are releases, on-call rotas and a support queue.

A two-person consultancy promises a report by the end of the month. Its operations are a calendar, a few freelancers and the founder's evenings.

Different work entirely. But in each case, someone has to know what's been promised, someone has to own getting it done, the work has to move from start to finish, someone needs to know whether it's on track, and something needs to change when it keeps going wrong.

That's the shape of this programme.

[ON SCREEN: "What operations is not"]

Two things operations is not.

It is not just the back office. Promises are made by sales and by founders. If operations isn't connected to what's being sold, it will always be catching up.

And it is not the same as process. Process is one tool. Some operational problems are solved by removing a process, or an approval step, or a tool nobody uses.

[ON SCREEN: exercise prompt]

Before the next lesson, take two minutes and write down three promises your business makes to customers. Not the ones on your website. The ones customers would actually complain about if you broke them. We'll use them in the next lesson.

**Slide and visual notes**

Title; definition; four boxes; three businesses comparison; what operations is not; prompt.

### 1.2 Promises, margin and cash

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

How a single promise flows into capacity, cost and cash timing, and why operational decisions are commercial ones. A first look at obligations.

**Estimated length**

About 6 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Promises, margin and cash"]

In the last lesson you wrote down three promises your business makes. In this one, we'll follow one of them through the business, because that's where operations stops being abstract.

[ON SCREEN: the bakery promise "Orders by 2pm, delivered by 7am next day"]

Back to the bakery. Ade promises cafés that any order placed by 2pm arrives by 7am the next morning.

Follow that promise.

**Capacity.** Baking starts at 3am. That needs two bakers, six days a week. One driver. If a baker is ill, someone covers, and in a small business that someone is usually the founder. So the promise quietly includes "Ade is available to bake at 3am".

**Cost and margin.** A rush order at 1.55pm for 40 extra loaves means overtime, or a second delivery run. If the price is the same as a normal order, the margin on that order might be close to nothing. The promise is being kept, but it's losing money.

**Cash.** Cafés pay on 30-day terms. The flour supplier wants paying in 14. When orders grow, Ade pays for more flour before the money for the extra bread arrives. Growth makes cash tighter, not looser. Plenty of businesses that are profitable on paper run short of cash for exactly this reason.

[ON SCREEN: Promise → capacity → cost → cash]

This is why operational decisions are commercial decisions. When someone agrees a delivery date, a discount, a payment term or a "we'll just add that in", they're making an operational commitment and a financial one at the same time.

You don't need to be an accountant to ask three questions about any promise:

[ON SCREEN: three questions]

- Who has to do what, and do they have the time?
- What does keeping this promise cost us, and does the price cover it?
- When do we pay out, and when do we get paid?

If nobody in the business can answer those for your main promises, that's useful information. It tells you where visibility is missing, which we'll come back to in Module 6.

[ON SCREEN: "Obligations"]

There's one more kind of promise to recognise early. Some promises aren't made to customers. They're obligations the business has because of where it operates, what sector it's in and how it's set up. Filing deadlines, tax returns, insurance renewals, licences, health and safety, data protection, employment terms.

This course will not tell you which of these apply to you. They depend on your jurisdiction, your sector and your company structure, and getting them wrong can be expensive. What we'll teach is how to recognise that an obligation exists, how to give it an owner and a deadline, how to keep evidence, and when to get qualified advice. That starts in Module 4.

[ON SCREEN: summary]

So, to recap the first module. Operations is how the business keeps its promises, reliably, at a cost it can afford. It holds promises, work, people and money together. And every promise has a capacity, a cost and a cash consequence, whether or not anyone has worked them out.

The exercise for this module asks you to map that for your own business. It takes about 25 minutes. You don't need to share it with anyone.

**Slide and visual notes**

Promise card; flow diagram; three questions; obligations; recap.

## Module 2: How work moves through a business

### 2.1 Triggers, handoffs and queues

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

The anatomy of a workflow, and why most delay is waiting rather than working.

**Estimated length**

About 6 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Triggers, handoffs and queues"]

In Module 1 we said operations is how the business keeps its promises. Now we're going to look at how the work behind a promise actually moves. Because if you can't see how work moves, you'll keep fixing the wrong thing.

[ON SCREEN: a simple flow — trigger → steps → result]

Every piece of work has the same basic shape.

There's a **trigger**: something that starts it. An order arrives. A client signs. A form is submitted. The first of the month comes round.

There are **steps**: the things people, or systems, actually do.

And there's a **result**: the thing the customer or the business receives. A delivery, a report, a paid invoice.

Between the steps there are three things worth naming.

[ON SCREEN: handoff icon]

**Handoffs.** Any time work passes from one person to another, or from you to a supplier, or from one system to another, that's a handoff. Handoffs are where work gets lost, misunderstood or forgotten. "I thought you'd sent it." "Nobody told me it had arrived."

[ON SCREEN: queue icon]

**Queues.** Work waiting for someone to pick it up. An inbox, a pile, a task list, a "to approve" folder. Queues are often invisible. Nobody sees a refund sitting in someone's inbox for three days. They only see the customer complaint.

[ON SCREEN: decision diamond]

**Decision points.** Places where someone has to choose. Approve or reject. Standard or custom. Which supplier. And approvals are a special kind of decision point, where the work can't continue until a named person says yes.

[ON SCREEN: the skincare refund table]

Here's what this looks like in practice. An online skincare brand, three staff and a fulfilment warehouse. Refunds were taking nine days. The founder assumed customer service was slow.

When they followed one refund through, the actual work took about twenty minutes. The other eight and a half days were waiting. Waiting for the post, which they can't control. Waiting for customer service to check the warehouse portal, which they only did twice a week. And waiting two to four days for the founder to approve any refund over fifty pounds.

[ON SCREEN: highlight founder approval]

That last one is worth pausing on. The founder had become a queue.

That happens in a lot of businesses, usually for a good reason at the time. There was a fraud problem once. A big refund went out by mistake. So the founder added an approval. Then the business grew, the approvals grew with it, and nobody went back to ask whether the control was still worth the delay.

[ON SCREEN: "Most delay is waiting, not working"]

Here's the general point. In most businesses, most of the time a piece of work spends in the system is waiting, not being worked on. So when work is slow, "work harder" and "hire someone" are often the wrong answers. The better question is: where does it wait, and why?

[ON SCREEN: three questions]

When you look at any piece of work, ask:

- What triggers it, and who knows it has started?
- Where does it change hands, and how does the next person find out?
- Where does it wait, for whom, and for how long?

In lesson 2.3 we'll turn those questions into a simple mapping method. First, though, we need to recognise that not all work is the same kind of work. That's the next lesson.

**Slide and visual notes**

Flow shape; handoff, queue, decision icons; refund table; founder as queue; three questions.

### 2.2 Recurring work, projects and exceptions

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

Three kinds of work that need different handling, and why treating everything as an exception brings it back to the founder.

**Estimated length**

About 5 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Recurring work, projects and exceptions"]

Founders often tell me "everything we do is different". Sometimes that's true. More often, there's a recurring core with variations on top, and the variations get so much attention that the core never gets organised.

It helps to sort work into three kinds.

[ON SCREEN: Recurring work]

**Recurring work** happens again and again in broadly the same way. Processing orders. Onboarding a new client. Running payroll. Sending invoices. Booking appointments. The steps are known. The value comes from doing it the same way each time, quickly and correctly.

Recurring work is where documentation, checklists, templates and clear ownership pay back fastest. If you do something forty times a month, a ten-minute improvement saves you most of a day.

[ON SCREEN: Project work]

**Project work** happens once, or rarely, with a defined end. Launching a new product. Moving office. Building a client's website. Implementing a new system. Each project is different, but projects share a shape: a scope, a plan, an owner, a deadline, and regular check-ins.

Project work needs a named owner and a clear scope more than it needs a detailed procedure. A project with no owner becomes the founder's project by default.

[ON SCREEN: Exceptions]

**Exceptions** are the things that don't fit. A customer asks for something you've never done. A supplier fails. A complaint escalates. A one-off discount request.

Exceptions need judgement, and a clear route for who makes it. Exceptions are where founders are often genuinely needed, at least at first.

[ON SCREEN: "When everything is an exception, everything comes to you"]

Here's the trap. If recurring work isn't organised, it starts to look like a series of exceptions. Every new client onboarding feels different because nobody has written down the common parts. Every order with a slight variation goes to the founder. And because exceptions need judgement, and the founder has the judgement, everything comes back to the founder.

So one of the most useful questions you can ask is: which of this is actually recurring? Usually it's more than people think.

[ON SCREEN: electrical contractor example]

An example. An electrical contractor with twelve staff said every quote was bespoke, so the founder priced every one. When they looked at the last three months, about seventy per cent of quotes were for four standard job types: consumer unit replacements, rewires of particular property sizes, EV charger installs and fault-finding call-outs. They were bespoke in detail, but not in structure.

That didn't mean the founder could stop pricing all quotes overnight. It meant they could separate the recurring quotes, which could follow a pricing guide and be prepared by someone else, from the genuinely unusual ones, which still needed the founder's eye.

[ON SCREEN: summary of the three kinds]

So when you map work in the next lesson, label it. Recurring, project or exception. It changes what good looks like. Recurring work wants consistency. Projects want an owner and a plan. Exceptions want a clear route to the right person, and that person shouldn't automatically be you.

**Slide and visual notes**

Three cards; trap statement; contractor quote split; summary.

### 2.3 Mapping a workflow without a consultant

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

A practical, observation-based method for mapping one workflow.

**Estimated length**

About 6 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Mapping a workflow without a consultant"]

You don't need software, sticky notes or a consultant to map a workflow. You need one piece of real work, a table and a bit of patience.

Here's the method.

[ON SCREEN: Step 1 — choose]

**Step one: choose one workflow.** Pick something that matters to a customer promise and happens often enough to observe. Order to delivery. Enquiry to signed client. New starter to fully set up. Month-end to invoices sent. Don't pick the whole business. Pick one path.

[ON SCREEN: Step 2 — follow real work]

**Step two: follow a real example, not your memory of it.** This is the most important step. When you map from memory, you describe how work is supposed to go. When you follow a real order, or a real client, you see how it actually goes. If you can, follow two or three examples, including one that went badly.

You can follow work by watching it, by looking at timestamps on emails and systems, or by asking the people involved to note when they received something and when they passed it on. Ask them. Don't guess what they do.

[ON SCREEN: the template columns]

**Step three: record each step in a table.** For each step write down:

- what happens,
- who does it,
- what they need to start (the input),
- what they produce (the output),
- what it waits for before the next step,
- and roughly how long it waits.

Mark anything that's a decision, and especially any approval. Mark every handoff. Mark anywhere you appear.

[ON SCREEN: Step 4 — label]

**Step four: label the kind of work.** Is this path recurring, a project, or an exception? If it's recurring, where do the exceptions branch off?

[ON SCREEN: Step 5 — look for patterns]

**Step five: look for patterns before solutions.** Where is the longest wait? Where does work change hands without anyone confirming it arrived? Where does it wait for one specific person? How many steps exist only because something went wrong once?

[ON SCREEN: a warning sign]

Two cautions.

First, the people who do the work know things you don't. If your map makes their job look simple and they look surprised, you've missed something. Map with them, not about them.

Second, resist fixing as you map. You'll see things that look obviously wrong. Write them down as questions. "Why does every refund over fifty pounds need approval?" There may be a reason you've forgotten. You'll choose what to change in Module 6, once you've seen the whole picture.

[ON SCREEN: what good output looks like]

What you end up with is a one-page table that shows how one piece of work really moves through your business, where it waits and for whom. That's enough to have a much better conversation than "everything's slow".

The exercise for this module is to do exactly this for one workflow. If you're following real work, spread it over a week. The resource includes the table and the questions to ask at the end.

**Slide and visual notes**

Five steps; template columns; cautions; example output.

## Module 3: Why founders become the bottleneck

### 3.1 Five ways founders become the bottleneck

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

Decision, knowledge, quality-check, relationship and visibility bottlenecks: how each forms and what each tends to need.

**Estimated length**

About 7 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Five ways founders become the bottleneck"]

"Everything comes to me." It's the most common thing founders say in this programme, and it's true. But it isn't one problem. It's usually several, tangled together, and each needs a different response.

Here are five types. You'll recognise most of them.

[ON SCREEN: 1. Decision bottleneck]

**One: the decision bottleneck.** Work waits because only you can decide. Approve this price. Sign off this hire. Agree this refund. Choose between these two suppliers.

It usually forms for a good reason. Early on, you were the only person with the context. Then the business grew and the decisions kept coming to you, because nobody ever said who else could make them.

What it tends to need: explicit decision rights, thresholds and an escalation route. We'll build those in Module 4.

[ON SCREEN: 2. Knowledge bottleneck]

**Two: the knowledge bottleneck.** Work waits because only you know how, or only you know a fact. Which supplier gives the discount. How the pricing spreadsheet really works. What a particular client is sensitive about.

What it tends to need: the knowledge written down or taught, and someone made responsible for keeping it current. That's where documentation fits, and it's Module 5.

[ON SCREEN: 3. Quality-check bottleneck]

**Three: the quality-check bottleneck.** The work is done by others, but you check it before it goes out. Every proposal. Every job sheet. Every social post.

Often this started after something went wrong. A costly mistake, a complaint, a compliance near-miss. Checking everything felt like the safe response.

What it tends to need: a clear standard, so people know what good looks like; checks built into the work itself, like a checklist; and sampling, where you check some rather than all. Some checks genuinely need to stay, especially where there are legal or safety consequences. The question is which ones, and whether you're the right person to do them.

[ON SCREEN: 4. Relationship bottleneck]

**Four: the relationship bottleneck.** Customers, suppliers or partners will only deal with you. The big client who emails you directly. The supplier who gives you a better price because they know you.

What it tends to need: planned, deliberate introductions. Agreeing with the client who their day-to-day contact is, and what you'll stay involved in. Relationships don't transfer because you've hired someone; they transfer when the client sees someone else being reliable.

[ON SCREEN: 5. Visibility bottleneck]

**Five: the visibility bottleneck.** You're the only one who can tell whether things are all right. So you check. You ask for updates. You look at the bank balance every morning. And because you're checking, people wait for you to notice problems instead of raising them.

What it tends to need: a small amount of shared information that shows status without you asking. That's Module 6.

[ON SCREEN: the electrical contractor table]

Let me show you how this looks in a real situation. An electrical contractor, twelve staff. The founder, Marcus, works sixty-five hours a week and thinks he needs an operations manager.

When we separate the types: he approves every quote over five hundred pounds, which is a decision bottleneck. He's the only one who knows supplier discounts: knowledge. He checks every job sheet before invoicing because of a paperwork error once: quality check. Three big landlord clients only deal with him: relationship. And he finds out which jobs are late by asking on the radio: visibility.

Five different bottlenecks. Five different responses.

[ON SCREEN: "Who receives the authority, information and relationships?"]

Now, would an operations manager help? Possibly. But only if that person receives the decision rights, the supplier knowledge and the client relationships. If they don't, Marcus now has a highly paid person waiting for him too.

[ON SCREEN: "A bottleneck by choice"]

One last point. Not every bottleneck is a problem. A founder can quite properly be the person who approves a major contract, or makes senior hires. The aim isn't for you to decide nothing. It's for you to be the bottleneck only where you've chosen to be, for good reasons, rather than by default.

**Slide and visual notes**

Five type cards; contractor table; authority/information/relationships; bottleneck by choice.

### 3.2 Asking diagnostic questions

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

How to separate symptoms from causes, and how to notice the information you don't yet have.

**Estimated length**

About 6 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Asking diagnostic questions"]

The most expensive operational mistakes I see aren't bad solutions. They're good solutions to the wrong problem. A new project tool when the issue was unclear ownership. A rewritten process when the issue was that one person didn't have the authority to say no.

The way to avoid that is to ask better questions before choosing an answer.

[ON SCREEN: Symptom → possible causes]

Start by separating the **symptom** from its possible **causes**.

A symptom is what you notice. "Projects are late." "Customers complain about response times." "We keep running out of stock."

A cause is why it happens. And any symptom usually has several possible causes.

Take "projects are late". Possible causes include: the deadline was never realistic; the scope grew without anyone agreeing it; work waited for an approval; a supplier was late; the person doing it was also doing three other things; nobody knew it was late until the end.

Each cause needs a completely different response. So your first job is to find out which ones are actually operating.

[ON SCREEN: four kinds of question]

Four kinds of question help.

**Questions about the path.** Where did this piece of work go, step by step? Where did it wait, and for whom? Your workflow map from Module 2 is exactly this.

**Questions about ownership.** Who owned the outcome? Not who did a task, but who was responsible for it being finished. Could they decide what they needed to, or did they have to wait?

**Questions about the promise.** What exactly was promised, by whom, and when? Was it checked against capacity before it was made?

**Questions about evidence.** How do we know? Is this one case or a pattern? What would we expect to see if this cause were true?

[ON SCREEN: "What information is missing?"]

Then ask the question most people skip: **what information is missing?**

Often the honest answer to "why are projects late?" is "we don't actually know". We don't record when work starts. We don't know how long approvals take. We don't know whether the original estimates were ever realistic.

That's not a failure. It's a finding. It tells you that before you change anything, you may need to look, or to start recording one or two things for a few weeks.

[ON SCREEN: "The first answer is rarely the cause"]

A useful habit: when someone gives you the cause, ask "and why did that happen?" once or twice more.

"The project was late because the designer was late." Why? "Because the client sent the content late." Why? "Because nobody told them the date it was needed, and nobody followed up." Now you're looking at a handoff and an ownership gap, not a slow designer.

Be careful with this. It's not about blaming people. If the answer keeps landing on an individual, ask what in the way work is set up made that likely.

[ON SCREEN: good versus weak questions]

Here's the difference between weak and strong diagnostic questions.

Weak: "Why is everyone so slow?" It assumes the cause and puts people on the defensive.

Stronger: "For the last three projects that ran late, where did the time go between the start date and delivery?"

Weak: "Do we need a new system?"

Stronger: "What decision would we make differently if we could see project status every day? Who would see it?"

[ON SCREEN: exercise prompt]

In the exercise, you'll take your own situation, or a provided case if you prefer, and write a diagnosis: which bottleneck types you see, the evidence for each, the questions you'd ask, and the information you're missing. You'll use exactly this approach in the capstone.

**Slide and visual notes**

Symptom/causes tree; four question kinds; missing information; why-chain; weak vs strong.

## Module 4: Ownership, decisions and when to get advice

### 4.1 Owners, decision rights and escalation

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

Owning an outcome versus doing a task; decide, consult and inform; escalation that is defined rather than improvised.

**Estimated length**

About 7 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Owners, decision rights and escalation"]

In Module 3, the most common bottleneck was decisions. Work waiting because only the founder can say yes. In this lesson, we'll look at how to move decisions to the right people without losing your grip on the ones that matter.

[ON SCREEN: Task vs outcome]

Start with a distinction. **Doing a task** and **owning an outcome** are different.

Doing a task: "Send the invoices." Owning an outcome: "Every client is invoiced correctly within three working days of month end, and you tell me if something stops that happening."

When you delegate a task, you still own the outcome. So you still have to check, chase and decide. When you delegate an outcome, the owner is the one who notices problems, makes the decisions within their limits, and comes to you only when they need to.

Most founders think they've delegated when they've only handed over tasks. Then they wonder why everything still comes back to them.

[ON SCREEN: Decision rights — Decide / Consult / Inform]

An owner needs **decision rights**: clarity about which decisions they can make. A simple way to set them is three words.

**Decide**: who makes the call.
**Consult**: who must be asked first, and whose view matters, but who doesn't decide.
**Inform**: who needs to know afterwards.

Take a refund over fifty pounds in our skincare business. Customer service lead decides, up to an agreed amount. Consult: nobody, for standard returns. Inform: the founder sees a weekly list of refunds. That's very different from "everything over fifty pounds waits for Priya".

[ON SCREEN: "One decider"]

One rule that saves a lot of trouble: **one decider per decision**. Many people can be consulted. If two people both think they decide, either nothing happens or two contradictory things happen.

[ON SCREEN: Escalation]

Next, **escalation**. Even with clear decision rights, some things need to move up. The trouble is that in most businesses escalation is improvised. People either escalate everything, to be safe, or nothing, until it's a crisis.

Defined escalation answers three questions:

- **When** does this go up? For example: the cost is over a threshold; a customer is threatening to leave; there's a safety, legal or regulatory concern; the owner and someone else disagree and can't resolve it.
- **To whom?** A named role, not "management".
- **How quickly, and how?** "Same day, by phone" is different from "at the weekly meeting".

[ON SCREEN: "Escalation is not failure"]

Make it clear that escalating within those rules is doing the job well, not admitting failure. If people are criticised for escalating, they stop, and you find out about problems late.

[ON SCREEN: small business example]

This isn't only for larger teams. A sole-founder consultancy with a virtual assistant and three freelancers can say: the assistant decides on diary changes and supplier payments under two hundred pounds; consults me on anything client-facing; tells me weekly what's been paid. A freelancer owns their deliverable to the agreed brief; escalates the same day if the client asks for anything outside it.

That's a decision rights table. It fits on one page, and it removes dozens of small interruptions a week.

[ON SCREEN: "Capability"]

One caution. An owner must be able to do what they own. If someone doesn't have the skill, the information or the time, giving them the title doesn't make them the owner. Part of setting ownership is checking that, and sometimes the honest answer is "not yet". That's fine, as long as you say so and plan for it.

In the next lesson, we'll look at the specific kind of decision right that causes most founder bottlenecks: approvals, and how to set thresholds for them.

**Slide and visual notes**

Task vs outcome; decide/consult/inform; one decider; escalation three questions; small business table; capability.

### 4.2 Approval thresholds and decision records

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

Delegated authority in plain terms; setting thresholds; recording the decisions that matter, lightly.

**Estimated length**

About 6 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Approval thresholds and decision records"]

This lesson is about governance. That word puts people off, so let's define it simply.

[ON SCREEN: "Governance: who can decide what, and how we know what was decided"]

For the purposes of this course, governance means **who can decide what, and how we know what was decided**. Every business has governance. In many small businesses it's accidental: the founder decides everything, and the record is in the founder's head.

There's a formal side too, to do with directors, shareholders and company law. That depends on your company structure and jurisdiction and isn't covered here. What we're covering is the everyday side, which you can design yourself.

[ON SCREEN: Delegated authority]

**Delegated authority** means a person can commit the business to something on its behalf, within limits. Spending money. Agreeing a discount. Signing a supplier order. Agreeing a delivery date. Hiring.

The limits are usually set as **approval thresholds**: below this amount or risk, you can decide; above it, you need approval from a named person.

[ON SCREEN: a simple threshold table]

Here's a simple example for a small business.

Purchases: team leads up to five hundred pounds; operations lead up to two thousand five hundred; founder above that.
Discounts: sales up to ten per cent; founder above that.
Refunds: customer service lead up to two hundred pounds; above that, operations lead.
Delivery commitments outside standard terms: must be confirmed in writing by the delivery lead before they're offered.
Any contract with non-standard terms, or over a set value: founder, after specialist review where needed.

[ON SCREEN: how to set a threshold]

How do you choose the numbers? Three questions:

- **What's the realistic downside if this person gets it wrong?** If the worst case is a small, recoverable loss, the threshold can be generous.
- **How often does this decision come up?** Frequent, low-risk decisions are the ones that clog up a founder. Push those down first.
- **Can we check afterwards instead of before?** For many decisions, a weekly list you glance at is enough. Checking afterwards keeps the work moving.

Start more generous than feels comfortable for low-risk decisions, and review in a month. You'll learn more from a month of real decisions than from a long debate.

[ON SCREEN: "Too many approvals recreate the bottleneck"]

A warning. It's tempting to respond to every mistake with a new approval step. Each one adds a queue. If you end up with five approvals on a routine purchase, you haven't removed the bottleneck. You've moved it and multiplied it.

[ON SCREEN: Decision records]

Now, records. Not every decision needs writing down. But some do: anything that commits significant money, changes a customer promise, changes who owns something, accepts a risk, or might be questioned later.

A **decision record** can be very light. Five lines:

- What was decided.
- Who decided, and who was consulted.
- When.
- Why, in a sentence or two, including what options were rejected.
- When it will be reviewed, if at all.

Keep them in one place everyone who needs them can find. A shared document or a channel is enough.

[ON SCREEN: why it matters]

Why bother? Because six months later, someone will ask "why do we do it this way?" or "who agreed this?" Without a record, the answer is "ask the founder". With one, the business remembers on its own.

And for some decisions, a record may matter for other reasons, for example if a dispute arises or someone needs to show how a decision was made. Whether any particular record is legally required depends on your circumstances, and that's a question for a qualified adviser.

The exercise for this module asks you to build a short decision rights table. Before that, the last lesson covers the decisions that most often go wrong: commitments, obligations and knowing when to get advice.

### 4.3 Commitments, compliance and specialist advice

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

Checking a commitment before it is made; giving obligations owners and evidence; recognising when to get qualified advice.

**Estimated length**

About 7 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Commitments, compliance and specialist advice"]

Some of the most damaging operational problems are created in a single moment: when someone agrees to something the business can't deliver. This lesson is about recognising those moments, and knowing where operational judgement stops and specialist advice begins.

[ON SCREEN: the software example]

Here's an example. A software company. A salesperson closes a large deal by promising a custom integration within six weeks. The product team hears about it after the contract is signed. Their estimate is twelve weeks, and doing it means delaying a release that other customers were promised.

Nobody did anything dishonest. The salesperson wanted to win. The problem was that nobody owned the question "what can we promise about custom work?", and nothing required anyone to check.

[ON SCREEN: Before you commit]

So before a significant commitment is made, check five things. These come from Module 1.

- **Scope.** What exactly are we agreeing to deliver? What's not included?
- **Capacity.** Who will do it, when, and what will it displace?
- **Margin.** Does the price cover the real cost, including the time it will take?
- **Payment and cash.** When do we pay out, and when are we paid? Are there penalties or service credits if we're late?
- **Dependencies.** What do we rely on, such as a supplier, a subcontractor, a system or the customer themselves, and what happens if they let us down?

And one governance question: **is the person agreeing this allowed to?** That's the decision rights table from the last lesson.

[ON SCREEN: Obligations]

Now, obligations. Some commitments aren't made to customers at all. They come from where you operate, your sector and your structure. Tax and filings. Employment. Health and safety. Data protection. Licences. Insurance. Sector rules.

For this course, the operational skills are:

- **Recognise** that an obligation may exist. If you're not sure, that's a signal to ask.
- **Give it an owner.** One named person responsible for knowing the requirement and making sure it's met.
- **Track deadlines.** A shared calendar or register, not someone's memory.
- **Keep evidence.** Being able to show that something was done, when and by whom.
- **Know when to get advice.** Which brings us to the most important part.

[ON SCREEN: "A checklist is not compliance"]

A checklist can help you remember. It doesn't make you compliant. Whether you meet an obligation depends on the actual requirement, which varies by jurisdiction, sector and circumstance. This course won't tell you what the law requires, and you shouldn't treat anything here as legal advice.

[ON SCREEN: When to get specialist advice]

So when should you get qualified advice? Some signals:

- The decision involves a contract with non-standard terms, especially liability, indemnities, service credits, termination, intellectual property or data.
- It involves employing, dismissing or changing the terms of people.
- It involves a regulated activity, or a sector with its own rules.
- There's a dispute, or one seems likely.
- You'd be relying on your own reading of a law or regulation.
- The cost of getting it wrong is high or hard to undo.

The relevant adviser might be a solicitor, an accountant, an employment specialist, an insurance broker, or a sector body.

[ON SCREEN: what to gather]

The operational skill is arriving with a clear question and the right information. Before you ask, gather:

- What's being decided, by when, and who will decide.
- The documents involved: the draft agreement, the policy, the correspondence.
- The facts: dates, amounts, who said what.
- What you're worried about, in plain words.
- What you'd like to be able to do.

That makes advice faster, cheaper and more useful.

[ON SCREEN: "Operational judgement has limits"]

To close the module: your job as founder isn't to be the lawyer, the accountant and the compliance officer. It's to make sure commitments are checked before they're made, that obligations have owners, and that the business knows when it's out of its depth. Recognising that is itself good judgement.

The exercise gives you a scenario where a founder has already signed something the business can't deliver. You'll work through authority, capacity, commercial consequences and specialist input.

## Module 5: Documentation and SOPs that people use

### 5.1 What an SOP is, and what it cannot fix

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

Definitions of SOP, checklist, guide, policy and template; capturing versus designing; problems documentation cannot solve.

**Estimated length**

About 6 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "What an SOP is, and what it cannot fix"]

"We need SOPs." I hear it in almost every founder conversation. It's usually right, partly. But before you write one, it helps to know what an SOP is, what kinds of documentation there are, and what no document will ever fix.

[ON SCREEN: SOP definition]

**SOP** stands for standard operating procedure. In plain terms: a written description of how a recurring piece of work should be done, by whom, and to what standard, so that it's done consistently without someone having to ask.

A good SOP usually says:

- what it's for, and when it applies;
- who owns it, and who does the work;
- what's needed to start;
- the steps, including any decisions and who makes them;
- what "done" looks like;
- what to do if something goes wrong, and who to tell.

[ON SCREEN: five kinds of documentation]

But "SOP" gets used for several different things. It's useful to separate them.

A **checklist** is a short list of things to do or confirm. Best for work people already know how to do, where the risk is forgetting a step.

An **SOP** sets out a process, with owners, decisions and standards. Best for recurring work with handoffs or judgement.

A **guide** or how-to shows how to use a tool or carry out a technical task, often step by step with screenshots. Best for something done rarely, or by new people.

A **policy** states a rule or principle and why it exists. "We don't accept payment in cash." "Refunds over two hundred pounds go to the operations lead." Best for decisions and boundaries, not steps.

A **template** is a pre-built starting point. A proposal template. An onboarding email. Best for work where the output has a standard shape.

Many problems that founders try to solve with a long SOP are better solved with a checklist, a policy line or a template.

[ON SCREEN: Capturing vs designing]

Now the most important distinction in this module. **Capturing steps** and **designing a process** are different things.

Capturing means writing down what people currently do. It's useful. It preserves knowledge that's in one person's head.

But if the current way of working has problems, capturing it preserves those problems. If the step is "remember to send the forms", writing that down doesn't make anyone remember.

Designing means asking, before you write: is this the right way to do it? Where does it wait? Who decides? What triggers each step? Can we remove a step, an approval or a handoff? That's what you practised in Modules 2 and 4.

The order matters. Map it. Fix what's obviously broken. Then document the version that works.

[ON SCREEN: what an SOP cannot fix]

And there are problems no document fixes.

- **Unclear ownership.** If nobody owns the outcome, a document won't make anyone responsible.
- **Missing authority.** If the person following the SOP has to wait for approval anyway, the SOP just describes the wait.
- **Not enough capacity.** If there aren't enough hours or people, a better description of the work doesn't create time.
- **A broken tool or set-up.** If the booking system allows the wrong appointment length, that's a configuration problem.
- **A promise that can't be kept.** If sales has promised something delivery can't do, documenting delivery won't help.
- **People choosing not to follow it.** Sometimes for good reasons, because it's wrong or out of date. Ask why before you insist.

[ON SCREEN: clinic example summary]

A physiotherapy clinic asked reception to write an SOP for new-patient bookings, because patients kept arriving without forms. The SOP was accurate, and useless, because the real issue was that nothing triggered the forms to be sent, and the booking system allowed new-patient appointments to be too short. Once they fixed the trigger and the system, a one-page checklist was enough.

In the next lesson: how to decide whether something needs documenting at all, and how to keep documents alive once they exist.

### 5.2 When to document, and what kind

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

Criteria for deciding; matching format to the job; ownership, access and review dates; keeping documents current.

**Estimated length**

About 6 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "When to document, and what kind"]

Not everything needs documenting. In fact, documenting too much is one of the most common ways small businesses waste time on operations. So here's how to decide.

[ON SCREEN: five criteria]

A task is worth documenting when one or more of these is true:

- **It's frequent.** Done weekly or more, so small improvements add up.
- **It's risky.** Getting it wrong has legal, safety, financial or reputational consequences.
- **It's error-prone.** It keeps going wrong in the same way.
- **It depends on one person.** If they were off for two weeks, it wouldn't happen, or would happen badly.
- **It's done rarely but must be done right.** Year-end tasks, annual renewals, the backup restore you've never tested.

If none of those is true, it may not need a document. A quick conversation, or nothing at all, may be fine.

[ON SCREEN: match format to job]

Then choose the lightest format that does the job.

If people know how to do it but forget steps: a **checklist**.
If it involves handoffs, decisions or standards: an **SOP**.
If it's technical, rare or done by new people: a **guide**, possibly with screenshots.
If it's really a rule about who can decide what: a **policy line**, often in your decision rights table.
If the output always has the same shape: a **template**.

[ON SCREEN: three fields every document needs]

Every document, whatever the format, needs three things beyond its content.

**An owner.** One named person or role responsible for keeping it accurate. Not "the team". The owner doesn't have to write it, but they have to make sure it stays right.

**Access.** Who needs to read it, who can edit it, and can they find it when they need it? A perfect SOP in a folder nobody can find is the same as no SOP. And some documents contain information not everyone should see, such as personal data, pricing or security details. Decide who should have access deliberately.

**A review date.** When will someone check it's still right? For fast-changing work, maybe every three months. For stable work, once a year. Put the date on the document itself.

[ON SCREEN: keeping documents current]

How do documents stay current? Not by heroic annual reviews. By habits:

- When the process changes, the document changes in the same week. Make that part of the change, not an afterthought.
- When someone follows a document and finds it wrong, they tell the owner, and that's welcomed.
- When something goes wrong, check whether the document was wrong, missing or ignored. Each needs a different fix.
- Delete or archive what's no longer used. Out-of-date documents are dangerous because people trust them.

[ON SCREEN: who writes it?]

Who should write it? Usually the person who does the work, with the owner checking it against the standard. Founders often end up writing SOPs at weekends, which is a sign the work hasn't actually been handed over.

And a practical tip: write the first version quickly, have someone else follow it while you watch, and fix whatever they stumble on. That test teaches you more than any amount of editing.

In the next lesson, we'll look at where documentation should live, what an SOP hub is, and how to judge tools for it.

### 5.3 SOP hubs and documentation tools: judging fit

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

What an SOP hub is for, how to evaluate one, and how to judge documentation tools by the job they need to do.

**Estimated length**

About 7 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "SOP hubs and documentation tools"]

Two questions come up in almost every cohort: where should our SOPs live, and should we use one of the tools that records your screen and turns it into a guide? This lesson won't recommend a product. Products change, and what suits a ten-person design studio won't suit a care provider. Instead, it'll give you a way to judge.

[ON SCREEN: "An SOP hub is a place plus rules"]

An **SOP hub** is simply the agreed place where your operational documents live. It could be a folder in your shared drive, a page in a wiki or workspace tool, a section of your project management system, or a dedicated documentation product.

The place matters less than the rules around it. A useful hub has:

- **One agreed location**, so people don't have to ask where things are.
- **A simple structure**, usually by area of work or by workflow, that matches how people look for things.
- **Consistent names**, so a search finds the right thing.
- **An owner and a review date on every document.**
- **Access set deliberately**: who can read, who can edit.
- **A way to know what's current**, and old versions archived rather than left lying around.

Without the rules, a hub becomes a graveyard of half-finished documents, and people go back to asking the founder.

[ON SCREEN: evaluating a hub — five questions]

To evaluate your hub, or one you're considering, ask:

- **Can people find what they need in under a minute,** while they're doing the work?
- **Is it where people already work?** A hub in a tool nobody opens won't be used.
- **Does every document show an owner and a review date?**
- **Is access right?** Can new starters and contractors see what they need, and not what they shouldn't?
- **What does it cost to keep current?** In time as well as money.

[ON SCREEN: judge tools by the job]

Now tools. There are many kinds of documentation tool. Rather than compare brands, start with the **job** the documentation needs to do.

[ON SCREEN: job → suitable formats]

If the job is **"remind experienced people of the steps"**, a checklist in whatever tool you already use is often enough.

If the job is **"show a new person how to use a particular screen or system"**, a step-by-step guide with screenshots helps. This is where screen-capture guide tools come in. Tools of this kind, Scribe being a commonly mentioned example, record your clicks and screens as you do a task in a browser or application, and turn them into a step-by-step guide with screenshots that you can then edit. That can save a lot of time for the right job.

If the job is **"agree who does what, who decides, and what good looks like"**, you need a written SOP. A screen capture won't say why a step exists, who owns it, what the standard is, or what to do when something goes wrong. It describes clicks, not judgement.

If the job is **"set a rule"**, that's a policy line, and the tool barely matters.

[ON SCREEN: a caution on screen-capture guides]

Two cautions about screen-capture guides specifically. First, they go out of date whenever the software's screens change, which can be often, so they need an owner and a review date like anything else. Second, they capture whatever's on screen. Check that guides don't expose personal data, client information or passwords before sharing them.

Features and pricing for any specific product change frequently. Check the vendor's current information before you choose, and don't rely on a course recording for it.

[ON SCREEN: tool evaluation questions]

When you evaluate a documentation tool, ask:

- What job do we need it to do, and does it do that job well?
- Will people use it where they already work, or is it one more place to look?
- Can we set owners, review dates and access?
- How easy is it to keep current when things change?
- Can we get our content out if we stop using it?
- What does it cost, in money and in the time to maintain it?
- Does it handle any sensitive information appropriately for our circumstances?

[ON SCREEN: "Decide what and who before which tool"]

The principle for this whole module: decide **what** needs documenting and **who** owns it before you decide **which tool**. A tool can make good documentation easier to keep. It can't decide what good looks like.

The exercise asks you to apply all three lessons to one piece of your own work.

## Module 6: Seeing what is happening, and choosing a first improvement

### 6.1 Seeing what is happening without checking everything

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

The minimum visibility set across delivery, money, capacity and obligations; who sees what; leading and lagging signals.

**Estimated length**

About 7 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Seeing what's happening without checking everything"]

In Module 3, one of the five bottlenecks was visibility. The founder is the only person who can tell whether things are all right, so they keep checking, and everyone waits for them to notice. This lesson is about fixing that with as little reporting as possible.

[ON SCREEN: "What would change a decision?"]

Start with one question: **what would I, or the owner, do differently if we knew this?** If the answer is "nothing", you don't need to track it. That one question cuts most dashboards in half.

[ON SCREEN: four areas]

Then think in four areas. For most small businesses, a handful of signals across these is enough.

**Delivery.** Is work on track against what we promised? Useful signals: what's late, what's at risk, what's blocked and waiting for whom. For recurring work, perhaps the time from request to completion.

**Money.** Will we have the cash we need, and are we making money on the work? Useful signals: cash in the bank and expected over the next few weeks; what customers owe us and how overdue; what we owe; margin on recent jobs or clients, even roughly.

**Capacity.** Do we have the people and time for what's coming? Useful signals: who's overloaded; work coming in versus work being finished; known absences.

**Obligations.** Are we meeting what we must? Useful signals: upcoming deadlines and renewals, with an owner for each; anything overdue; where evidence is kept.

[ON SCREEN: Leading and lagging]

Within those, it helps to know the difference between **lagging** and **leading** signals.

A lagging signal tells you what already happened. Customer complaints. Last month's profit. A missed deadline. Useful, but late.

A leading signal warns you before it happens. Work waiting more than two days for approval. Invoices not sent within a week of delivery. A key person with no cover next month. These are the ones that let owners act early, before the founder has to step in.

[ON SCREEN: Who sees what, how often]

Then decide **who sees what, and how often**. The owner of an outcome should see the signals for it, frequently enough to act. The founder might see a short summary weekly. Some things, like cash, may need a weekly look. Others, like obligations, might be a monthly check of what's coming up.

[ON SCREEN: an example]

Here's what a minimum visibility set might look like for a nine-person recruitment firm:

- Placements starting this week, and any with paperwork incomplete. Owner: consultant lead. Daily.
- Days from start date to invoice sent. Owner: finance assistant. Weekly.
- Cash now, and expected over four weeks. Owner: founder. Weekly.
- Overdue invoices over thirty days. Owner: finance assistant. Weekly.
- Obligation deadlines in the next sixty days. Owner: operations lead. Monthly.

Five lines. One shared sheet. The founders stop asking for updates because the updates are already visible.

[ON SCREEN: cautions]

Three cautions.

First, a number is only useful if someone owns it and will act on it.

Second, people will improve the number, sometimes in ways you didn't intend. If you measure only speed, quality may slip. Pair a speed measure with a quality check.

Third, don't build a big reporting system before you know which signals matter. Start with a shared sheet, use it for a month, and keep only what people actually looked at.

[ON SCREEN: link to improvement]

Visibility also does something else: it gives you a baseline. And you need a baseline to know whether an improvement worked. That's the next lesson.

### 6.2 Choosing a sensible first improvement

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**What this lesson covers**

Turning diagnosis into candidates; prioritising by impact, frequency, risk and effort; owner, measure, review and stop rule; what success can and cannot prove.

**Estimated length**

About 7 minutes

**Recording script**

[ON SCREEN: "Choosing a sensible first improvement"]

By now you've mapped a workflow, diagnosed your bottlenecks, looked at ownership and decisions, considered documentation and thought about visibility. You probably have a long list of things you'd like to change. This lesson is about choosing one, and being able to tell whether it worked.

[ON SCREEN: "One at a time"]

Why one? Three reasons.

If you change several things at once, you can't tell which helped. The people doing the work have to absorb every change on top of their day jobs. And founders who start five improvements tend to finish none, which pulls them back in as the project manager of everything.

One at a time doesn't mean slowly. A good first improvement might take a few weeks.

[ON SCREEN: candidates from diagnosis]

Start by turning your diagnosis into **candidates**. Each candidate should name the problem it addresses and the bottleneck type. "Introduce a pricing guide for standard quotes, to reduce the decision bottleneck on quotes." Not just "pricing guide".

[ON SCREEN: four criteria]

Then compare candidates on four things.

**Impact.** If it works, how much difference will it make to a customer promise, to cash, or to the founder's time? Be specific.

**Frequency.** How often does the problem occur? A problem that happens every day usually beats one that happens twice a year, unless the rare one is very serious.

**Risk.** What happens if we leave it? And what's the risk of the change itself going wrong? Changes involving legal, safety or regulatory obligations need more care, and sometimes specialist input before anything changes.

**Effort.** How much time, money and disruption? Who has to do the work?

[ON SCREEN: recruitment firm table]

Here's the recruitment firm again. Three candidates. A new CRM: unclear impact, high effort, and nobody can say what problem it solves. Faster invoicing, owned by the finance assistant: high impact on cash, happens on every placement, low effort. Moving pre-employment paperwork checks from the founder to a trained consultant: high impact, but it involves legal obligations, so the requirements and who may carry out the checks need confirming with a qualified adviser first.

They chose faster invoicing first. The paperwork change matters more in the long run, so they started the preparation, getting advice and training, in parallel, but didn't change anything until that was done. The CRM was parked.

That's a defensible choice. Another team might reasonably have prioritised differently. What matters is that the reasoning is explicit.

[ON SCREEN: the brief]

Once you've chosen, write a short brief. Five things:

- **Owner.** One person responsible for making the change happen. Ideally not the founder.
- **The change.** What will be different, in a sentence or two.
- **The measure and baseline.** What number or observation will tell you whether it worked, and what it is now. If you don't have a baseline, spend a week or two getting one.
- **A review date.** When you'll look at the results together.
- **A stop rule.** What would make you pause or reverse it. For example, if errors rise or customers complain.

Add one more line: **what else to watch**, so improving one thing doesn't quietly break another.

[ON SCREEN: what success can and cannot show]

Finally, be honest about what success shows. If invoicing gets faster, that shows invoicing got faster. It doesn't show the business is fixed, or that every other idea on the list was wrong.

And there's an important difference for this course specifically. In the capstone, you'll reason through a hypothetical business. That demonstrates your judgement. It doesn't demonstrate that you've made a change in your own business, or that it worked. Those are different kinds of evidence, and we keep them separate.

[ON SCREEN: close of course]

That's the end of the six modules. You now have a working vocabulary, a way of seeing how work moves, a way of diagnosing why it depends on you, and a way of choosing and testing one improvement. The capstone brings it all together on a business that isn't yours, so you can practise judgement without the pressure of real consequences. Good luck with it.
