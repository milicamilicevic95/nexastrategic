# Course outline: Operations Fundamentals

_Operations Academy (working name). Exported from Nexa OS on 21 October 2026. Current drafts: nothing is approved unless its status says so._

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Pillars:** Direction; Ownership; Delivery; Visibility; Improvement

**Purpose**

Give founders a shared operational vocabulary and enough judgement to diagnose why work depends on them, ask better questions, and choose a sensible first improvement.

The course answers one question: **how do you remove yourself as the bottleneck in productivity?** It does not answer it for each learner's business. It gives them the concepts and methods to answer it for themselves, and to judge advice and tools they are offered.

**Audience**

Founders and owner-managers of small and growing businesses, from sole traders with a few contractors to companies of around 50 people, who may have little formal operations knowledge. Intelligent, busy, experienced in their own field. No shared operational vocabulary is assumed.

**Subjects covered**

What operations is; how work moves; why founders become bottlenecks; ownership, decision rights and escalation; commitments and when to get specialist advice; documentation and SOPs; visibility; choosing and reviewing a first improvement. Governance, compliance and commercial awareness are introduced at a foundational level throughout.

**Prerequisites**

None. This course is the prerequisite for the rest of the programme.

**Estimated learning time**

Estimates only, to be checked in the pilot.

| Part | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Six modules, recordings | about 75 to 90 minutes in total |
| Six modules, exercises and resources | about 3 to 3.5 hours in total |
| Capstone case | 3 to 5 hours |
| **Total** | **about 7.5 to 10 hours**, spread over two to four weeks |

**Structure and rationale**

**Why six modules, not five.** The brief suggested about five. Topic 4 (foundational practices for ownership, documentation, delivery, visibility and responsible decision-making) is too large for one short module, and SOPs and documentation are the questions founders have already raised. It is split into Module 4 (ownership and responsible decisions) and Module 5 (documentation and SOPs). Visibility moves into Module 6, where it is needed to judge whether a first improvement has worked. Each module stays short enough for one sitting.

| Module | Brief topic |
|---|---|
| 1. What operations is, and why it is commercial | 1 |
| 2. How work moves through a business | 2 |
| 3. Why founders become the bottleneck | 3 |
| 4. Ownership, decisions and when to get advice | 4 (ownership, responsible decisions, governance, commercial and legal awareness) |
| 5. Documentation and SOPs that people use | 4 (documentation, delivery) |
| 6. Seeing what is happening and choosing a first improvement | 4 (visibility) and 5 |

**Not covered here**

Detailed finance, project management, governance, compliance and legal content. These have their own courses. Software training. The course mentions tools only to show how to judge whether one fits.

**Completion and evidence**

A learner completes the course by working through the six modules and submitting the capstone case. The capstone shows **reasoning about a hypothetical business**. It does not show that the learner has changed their own business, and it is not described as doing so. No certificate or accreditation is attached.

The fortnightly or monthly masterclasses (frequency to be agreed) are optional. Everything needed to complete the course is in the recordings, exercises and resources.

## Module 1: What operations is, and why it is commercial

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Learner problem**

Founders hear "operations" used to mean anything from admin to logistics to "everything that isn't sales". Without a working definition they cannot tell what they are trying to fix, and they treat operations as back-office tidying rather than the way the business keeps its promises and makes money.

**Pillars:** Direction; Visibility

**Prerequisite modules:** None

**Learning outcomes**

- Explain, in their own words, what operations covers in their business, giving one example each of a customer promise, a constraint and a recurring decision.
- Trace how one customer promise affects delivery capacity, margin and the timing of cash in and out.

**Lessons**

- 1.1 Operations is how the business keeps its promises (About 6 minutes). A working definition of operations, and the four things it holds together: promises, work, people and money.
- 1.2 Promises, margin and cash (About 6 minutes). How a single promise flows into capacity, cost and cash timing, and why operational decisions are commercial ones. A first look at obligations.

**Exercises and resources**

- Exercise: Exercise 1: Operations scope map
- Resource: Operations scope map

**Lesson outline**

1. **Lesson 1.1: Operations is how the business keeps its promises** (about 6 minutes). A working definition. The four things operations has to hold together: promises, work, people and money. Why operations looks different in a bakery, a software company and a consultancy, and what stays the same.
2. **Lesson 1.2: Promises, margin and cash** (about 6 minutes). How one customer promise flows through capacity, cost and cash timing. Why operations decisions are commercial decisions. A first look at obligations the business has to meet.
3. **Exercise 1:** Operations scope map for your own business (about 25 minutes).

**Worked example**

**A wholesale bakery supplying 30 independent cafés.**

The founder, Ade, promises next-morning delivery for any order placed by 2pm. That one promise sets a lot of operations:

- **Work.** Baking has to start at 3am with yesterday afternoon's order list. A driver route has to be planned by 5am.
- **People.** Two bakers and one driver must be available six days a week. When one is ill, Ade covers.
- **Suppliers.** Flour and butter must be in stock, because there is no time to order for tomorrow.
- **Money.** Cafés pay on 30-day terms. The flour supplier wants payment in 14 days. Growth means paying for more ingredients before the money for the extra bread arrives.
- **Obligations.** Food safety and allergen information have to be right on every product. (The specific requirements depend on jurisdiction and need checking with the relevant authority or an adviser. The course does not state them.)

When Ade says "operations is a mess", she might mean any of these. The useful question is which promise is under strain, and where.

**Common misconceptions**

- **"Operations is admin."** Admin is part of it. Operations is the whole system that turns a promise into a delivered result and the money into the bank.
- **"Operations is something you sort out once you're bigger."** A sole trader has operations. They are just all in one head.
- **"Good operations means more process."** Sometimes it means fewer steps, fewer approvals and fewer tools.
- **"Every business needs the same operations functions."** A 6-person bakery and an 18-person software company need very different things. The questions are the same; the answers are not.

**Estimated learning time**

Estimate: about 40 minutes (12 minutes of recordings, 25 minutes of exercise, a few minutes of reading).

## Module 2: How work moves through a business

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Learner problem**

Founders see the results of work (late, early, wrong, fine) but rarely the path work takes. Without seeing triggers, handoffs, queues and decision points, they fix the wrong thing, often by working harder or hiring.

**Pillars:** Delivery; Ownership

**Prerequisite modules:** What operations is, and why it is commercial

**Learning outcomes**

- Map one workflow from trigger to completion, marking each handoff, queue, decision point and approval.
- Distinguish recurring work, project work and exceptions in a given situation, and explain why each needs a different approach.
- Identify where work waits, and for whom, using observed evidence rather than impression.

**Lessons**

- 2.1 Triggers, handoffs and queues (About 6 minutes). The anatomy of a workflow, and why most delay is waiting rather than working.
- 2.2 Recurring work, projects and exceptions (About 5 minutes). Three kinds of work that need different handling, and why treating everything as an exception brings it back to the founder.
- 2.3 Mapping a workflow without a consultant (About 6 minutes). A practical, observation-based method for mapping one workflow.

**Exercises and resources**

- Exercise: Exercise 2: Map one workflow
- Resource: Workflow map

**Lesson outline**

1. **Lesson 2.1: Triggers, handoffs and queues** (about 6 minutes). The anatomy of a workflow. Why work spends most of its time waiting. Where founders sit in the flow.
2. **Lesson 2.2: Recurring work, projects and exceptions** (about 5 minutes). Three kinds of work that need different handling. Why treating everything as an exception brings it back to the founder.
3. **Lesson 2.3: Mapping a workflow without a consultant** (about 6 minutes). A practical method using one real piece of work, observed rather than remembered.
4. **Exercise 2:** Map one workflow (about 40 minutes, spread over a week if you are following real work).

**Worked example**

**An online skincare brand: three staff, a fulfilment warehouse (3PL) and the founder, Priya.**

Refunds take nine days on average. Customers complain. Priya assumes customer service is slow and considers hiring.

Mapping one refund from start to finish shows:

| Step | Who | Typical wait before the next step |
|---|---|---|
| Customer emails asking to return | Customer | – |
| Customer service replies with returns label | CS | 4 hours |
| Parcel arrives at the 3PL | 3PL | 3 days (post) |
| 3PL logs the return in its system | 3PL | 1 day |
| CS sees the return logged | CS | 1 day (checks the 3PL portal on Mondays and Thursdays) |
| Refund over £50 needs Priya's approval | Priya | **2 to 4 days** |
| CS issues refund | CS | Same day |

The work itself takes perhaps 20 minutes. The rest is waiting. Two waits are inside the business: the twice-weekly portal check and Priya's approval. Hiring would not fix either.

Better questions: why are refunds over £50 approved by the founder? What risk does that control protect against? Could the threshold change, or could a check happen afterwards instead of before? Could the 3PL notify CS rather than CS checking? (These are questions, not answers. Priya may have good reasons, for example a past fraud problem.)

**Common misconceptions**

- **"Slow work means slow people."** Most delay is waiting between steps, not doing the steps.
- **"We need a process map of the whole business."** Map one piece of work that matters. Whole-business maps take weeks and go out of date.
- **"Everything we do is bespoke."** Most businesses that say this have a recurring core with variations. Finding the core is the point.
- **"I'll map it from memory."** Memory describes how work should go. Following real work shows how it does go.

**Estimated learning time**

Estimate: about 60 minutes (17 minutes of recordings, 40 minutes of mapping).

## Module 3: Why founders become the bottleneck

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Learner problem**

Founders know they are overloaded but describe it as one problem ("everything comes to me"). Different bottlenecks need different responses. Without separating them, founders reach for the wrong fix: delegation without authority, documentation without ownership, hiring without visibility.

**Pillars:** Ownership; Delivery; Visibility

**Prerequisite modules:** What operations is, and why it is commercial; How work moves through a business

**Learning outcomes**

- Diagnose which bottleneck types (decision, knowledge, quality check, relationship, visibility) are operating in a scenario, citing evidence for each.
- Write diagnostic questions that separate a symptom from its likely causes and identify what information is missing before deciding.

**Lessons**

- 3.1 Five ways founders become the bottleneck (About 7 minutes). Decision, knowledge, quality-check, relationship and visibility bottlenecks: how each forms and what each tends to need.
- 3.2 Asking diagnostic questions (About 6 minutes). How to separate symptoms from causes, and how to notice the information you don't yet have.

**Exercises and resources**

- Exercise: Exercise 3: Bottleneck diagnosis
- Resource: Bottleneck diagnostic

**Lesson outline**

1. **Lesson 3.1: Five ways founders become the bottleneck** (about 7 minutes). Decision, knowledge, quality-check, relationship and visibility bottlenecks. How each one forms, and what each one tends to need.
2. **Lesson 3.2: Asking diagnostic questions** (about 6 minutes). Separating symptoms from causes. Questions that reveal missing information. Why the first answer is rarely the cause.
3. **Exercise 3:** Bottleneck diagnosis (about 35 minutes).

**Worked example**

**An electrical contracting firm: 12 staff, founder Marcus.**

Marcus works 65-hour weeks. He says he needs an operations manager. Using the five bottleneck types:

| Type | Evidence | Likely need |
|---|---|---|
| Decision | Every quote over £500 needs Marcus to approve the price. Staff wait 2 to 3 days. | Pricing guide for standard jobs, a threshold, a named second approver. |
| Knowledge | Only Marcus knows the supplier discounts and which wholesaler has stock. | Supplier information written down and owned by someone. |
| Quality check | Marcus reviews every job sheet before invoicing because a certification paperwork error was costly once. | A checklist at the point of work, spot checks instead of checking everything, and a clear owner for compliance paperwork. |
| Relationship | Three large landlord clients only deal with Marcus. | Planned introductions to a named account lead; agreeing with clients who their contact is. |
| Visibility | Marcus knows which jobs are late only by asking on the van radio. | A simple job board showing status, visible to the office. |

Hiring an operations manager might help, but only if that person is given decision rights, the supplier knowledge and the client relationships. Otherwise the new hire becomes another person waiting for Marcus.

The certification paperwork is also an obligation with legal and safety consequences. Changing who checks it is a **governance decision** that Marcus should make deliberately, and the specific requirements should be confirmed with the relevant scheme or a qualified adviser rather than assumed.

**Common misconceptions**

- **"The answer is delegation."** Delegation without decision rights, information or visibility moves the queue rather than removing it.
- **"I'm the bottleneck because I'm a control freak."** Sometimes. More often the business has grown around the founder's knowledge and nobody has redesigned it. Personal habits matter, and David's work addresses them, but structure usually matters at least as much.
- **"A new hire will fix it."** Only if the hire receives the authority, information and relationships that currently sit with the founder.
- **"All bottlenecks are bad."** A founder can rightly be the decision-maker for a few high-stakes decisions. The goal is that they are the bottleneck only where they choose to be.

**Estimated learning time**

Estimate: about 50 minutes (13 minutes of recordings, 35 minutes of exercise).

## Module 4: Ownership, decisions and when to get advice

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Learner problem**

Founders delegate tasks but not outcomes or authority, so decisions drift back to them. They also make, or allow others to make, commitments without checking capacity, margin, cash or legal consequences, and often do not know when a matter needs a qualified adviser.

**Pillars:** Ownership; Direction; Visibility

**Prerequisite modules:** Why founders become the bottleneck

**Learning outcomes**

- For a recurring decision or outcome, specify the owner, their decision rights, an approval threshold and the escalation route.
- Before a commitment is made, identify its consequences for scope, capacity, margin, payment and cash, and dependencies on suppliers or other customers.
- Recognise when a matter needs a qualified adviser, say what kind, and list the information to gather before asking.

**Lessons**

- 4.1 Owners, decision rights and escalation (About 7 minutes). Owning an outcome versus doing a task; decide, consult and inform; escalation that is defined rather than improvised.
- 4.2 Approval thresholds and decision records (About 6 minutes). Delegated authority in plain terms; setting thresholds; recording the decisions that matter, lightly.
- 4.3 Commitments, compliance and specialist advice (About 7 minutes). Checking a commitment before it is made; giving obligations owners and evidence; recognising when to get qualified advice.

**Exercises and resources**

- Exercise: Exercise 4: The 4-hour response promise
- Resource: Decision rights and thresholds
- Resource: Commitment check and specialist advice brief

**Lesson outline**

1. **Lesson 4.1: Owners, decision rights and escalation** (about 7 minutes). Owning an outcome versus doing a task. Decide, consult, inform. Escalation that is defined rather than improvised.
2. **Lesson 4.2: Approval thresholds and decision records** (about 6 minutes). Delegated authority in plain terms. Setting thresholds. Which decisions are worth recording, and how lightly.
3. **Lesson 4.3: Commitments, compliance and specialist advice** (about 7 minutes). Checking a commitment before it is made. Obligations need owners and evidence. Recognising when to get qualified advice and what to gather first.
4. **Exercise 4:** The 4-hour response promise (about 45 minutes).

**Worked example**

**A B2B software company: 18 staff.**

A salesperson closes a large deal by promising a custom integration "within six weeks". The product team learns about it after signature. They estimate twelve weeks, and the work displaces a scheduled release for existing customers.

Looking at it through this module:

- **Ownership.** Nobody owned "what we can promise about custom work". Sales assumed product would cope. Product assumed sales would ask.
- **Decision rights.** Sales could agree prices within a discount band, but nothing said who could agree delivery commitments or custom scope.
- **Commercial consequences.** The deal is profitable at twelve weeks of work only if the price included it, which it did not. Delaying the release may breach promises to other customers.
- **Specialist input.** The signed agreement may include delivery dates, service credits or termination rights. What they mean is a question for a solicitor, not for the founder reading it at midnight. The founder's job is to recognise that, gather the agreement and the facts, and ask a clear question.

A proportionate fix, which the company chose after the event: a short decision rights table saying sales may agree standard scope and discounts up to 10%; any custom work or delivery date needs written confirmation from the product lead before it is offered; anything over a set contract value or with non-standard terms goes to the founder; and agreements with non-standard liability, service credit or data terms are reviewed by their solicitor before signature.

**Common misconceptions**

- **"Delegating means giving someone the task."** It means giving them the outcome and the authority to make the decisions it needs, within limits.
- **"Approval steps make things safer."** Each approval also adds a queue. Good thresholds protect what matters and let the rest move.
- **"Governance is for big companies."** At its simplest, governance is just knowing who can decide what, and keeping a record of the decisions that matter. Every business has it, deliberately or by accident.
- **"I'll read the contract myself, it's only a few pages."** Reading it is good. Interpreting liability, termination, intellectual property or data terms needs a qualified adviser. The operational skill is knowing when, and arriving with the right information.
- **"If we have a checklist, we're compliant."** A checklist can help you remember. It does not establish that you meet an obligation.

**Estimated learning time**

Estimate: about 65 minutes (20 minutes of recordings, 45 minutes of exercise).

## Module 5: Documentation and SOPs that people use

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Learner problem**

Founders have been told they need SOPs and an SOP hub, and are being sold tools to make them. They are unsure what an SOP is, where it should live, what to document first, or how a screen-capture guide differs from other documentation. Many have written SOPs that nobody reads, or that describe a process nobody follows.

**Pillars:** Delivery; Ownership

**Prerequisite modules:** How work moves through a business; Ownership, decisions and when to get advice

**Learning outcomes**

- Decide whether a task needs documentation and what kind (none, checklist, SOP, guide, policy or template), using stated criteria.
- Distinguish capturing current steps from designing a workable process, and explain what an SOP cannot fix in a given situation.
- Evaluate an SOP hub or documentation tool against the job it needs to do, including ownership, access, review and the effort of keeping it current.

**Lessons**

- 5.1 What an SOP is, and what it cannot fix (About 6 minutes). Definitions of SOP, checklist, guide, policy and template; capturing versus designing; problems documentation cannot solve.
- 5.2 When to document, and what kind (About 6 minutes). Criteria for deciding; matching format to the job; ownership, access and review dates; keeping documents current.
- 5.3 SOP hubs and documentation tools: judging fit (About 7 minutes). What an SOP hub is for, how to evaluate one, and how to judge documentation tools by the job they need to do.

**Exercises and resources**

- Exercise: Exercise 5: Decide, design and evaluate
- Resource: Should this be documented? Decision guide and SOP template
- Resource: SOP hub and tool evaluation

**Lesson outline**

1. **Lesson 5.1: What an SOP is, and what it cannot fix** (about 6 minutes). Definitions: SOP, checklist, guide, policy, template. The difference between capturing current steps and designing a workable process. Problems documentation cannot solve.
2. **Lesson 5.2: When to document, and what kind** (about 6 minutes). Criteria for deciding. Matching the format to the job. Ownership, access and review dates. Keeping documents current.
3. **Lesson 5.3: SOP hubs and documentation tools: judging fit** (about 7 minutes). What an SOP hub is for. Evaluating a hub. Judging documentation tools, including screen-capture guide tools, against the job they need to do.
4. **Exercise 5:** Decide, design and evaluate (about 45 minutes).

**Worked example**

**A physiotherapy clinic: four practitioners, two reception staff, owner-practitioner Tom.**

New patients regularly arrive without forms completed, and appointments overrun. Tom asks reception to "write an SOP for new patient bookings".

Reception writes down what they currently do. It is accurate, and it does not help, because:

- **It captures the steps, but the process is the problem.** Forms are sent only when a receptionist remembers. Nothing triggers the send. Writing down "remember to send forms" documents the weakness.
- **Part of the problem is not a process at all.** The booking system allows a new-patient slot to be booked at the same length as a follow-up. That is a configuration decision, and documentation cannot fix it.
- **Some content is clinical or regulated.** How patient information is collected and stored may be subject to data protection and professional requirements. That part needs checking against the relevant guidance or with an adviser, and needs an owner who is accountable for it. It should not be decided by whoever writes the SOP.

What helped: Tom and reception redesigned the flow first (the booking system sends forms automatically at booking, with a reminder 48 hours before; new-patient slots set longer). **Then** they wrote a one-page checklist for the parts that still needed people, named the reception lead as owner, and set a review date three months out.

The tool question came last. A short written checklist in the shared drive was enough. A screen-capture guide was useful for one task only: the booking system's rarely used "transfer patient" screen.

**Common misconceptions**

- **"We need SOPs for everything."** Document what is frequent, risky, error-prone or dependent on one person. Much work needs no document at all.
- **"Writing down what we do is the same as fixing it."** Capturing current steps preserves current problems. Design first, then document.
- **"The tool will make us organised."** A tool stores and presents documents. It does not decide what should be documented, who owns it, or whether people follow it.
- **"An SOP hub is a place."** It is a place plus rules: ownership, access, naming, review dates and a way to find things.
- **"Once written, it's done."** An SOP without an owner and a review date goes out of date, and an out-of-date SOP is worse than none, because people trust it.
- **"A screen recording is an SOP."** A click-by-click guide explains how to use a screen. It usually does not say why, when, who decides, or what good looks like.

**Estimated learning time**

Estimate: about 65 minutes (19 minutes of recordings, 45 minutes of exercise).

## Module 6: Seeing what is happening, and choosing a first improvement

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Learner problem**

Founders stay involved because they are the only ones who can tell whether things are on track, and when they decide to improve operations they start several changes at once, choose by gut feel or software demo, and never check whether anything worked.

**Pillars:** Visibility; Improvement

**Prerequisite modules:** Why founders become the bottleneck; Ownership, decisions and when to get advice; Documentation and SOPs that people use

**Learning outcomes**

- Identify the minimum information needed to see whether delivery, money, capacity and obligations are on track, and who should see it and how often.
- Prioritise a first improvement from several candidates using impact, frequency, risk and effort, and justify the choice.
- Define an owner, a baseline and measure, a review date and a stop rule for an improvement, and state what success would and would not show.

**Lessons**

- 6.1 Seeing what is happening without checking everything (About 7 minutes). The minimum visibility set across delivery, money, capacity and obligations; who sees what; leading and lagging signals.
- 6.2 Choosing a sensible first improvement (About 7 minutes). Turning diagnosis into candidates; prioritising by impact, frequency, risk and effort; owner, measure, review and stop rule; what success can and cannot prove.

**Exercises and resources**

- Exercise: Exercise 6: Visibility set and first improvement brief
- Resource: Minimum visibility set
- Resource: First improvement brief

**Lesson outline**

1. **Lesson 6.1: Seeing what is happening without checking everything** (about 7 minutes). The minimum visibility set: delivery, money, capacity, obligations. Who needs to see what, how often. Leading and lagging signals. Avoiding dashboards nobody uses.
2. **Lesson 6.2: Choosing a sensible first improvement** (about 7 minutes). Turning diagnosis into candidates. Prioritising by impact, frequency, risk and effort. One owner, one measure, one review date, and a stop rule. What success can and cannot prove.
3. **Exercise 6:** Minimum visibility set and first improvement brief (about 50 minutes).

**Worked example**

**A recruitment firm: nine staff, two founders.** (The same business as the Module 3 practice case.)

After diagnosis, the founders have three candidate improvements:

| Candidate | Impact | Frequency | Risk if left | Effort | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. New CRM | Unclear | – | Low | High (months) | Solves a problem nobody has yet evidenced |
| B. Invoice placements within 2 days of start, owned by the finance assistant | High (cash arrives 2 to 3 weeks sooner) | Every placement | Medium (cash pressure) | Low | Needs a decision rights change: Sam stops invoicing personally |
| C. Pre-employment paperwork checks owned by a trained consultant, with Sam spot-checking 1 in 5 | High (start dates stop slipping) | Every placement | **High if done badly** | Medium | Involves legal obligations; the requirements and who may check need confirming with a qualified adviser first |

They choose **B first**. It is high-impact, frequent and low-effort, and its risk is low. C matters more in the long run but needs specialist input before anything changes. That work starts in parallel as **preparation**, not as a change. A is parked until there is evidence the current system is the constraint.

**First improvement brief for B:**
- **Owner:** finance assistant. **Sponsor:** Sam.
- **Change:** invoice within two working days of a confirmed start, using the existing template; Sam no longer issues invoices.
- **Measure:** median days from start date to invoice sent. Baseline: 16. Target: 2.
- **Also watch:** invoice errors (should not rise).
- **Review:** after 6 weeks.
- **Stop rule:** if errors rise above 1 in 10 or clients complain, pause and review.

What success will show: invoicing is faster. What it will not show: that the CRM was unnecessary, or that the firm is "fixed".

**Common misconceptions**

- **"We need a dashboard."** You need the few signals that would change a decision, seen by the people who can act. That might be one shared sheet.
- **"More data is safer."** Too much reporting hides the signal and creates work.
- **"Let's fix everything at once."** Several simultaneous changes make it impossible to tell which helped, and overload the people doing the work.
- **"Start with the biggest problem."** Sometimes. Often a smaller, faster change builds confidence and capacity for the big one. The point is to choose deliberately.
- **"It worked, so we're done."** Check whether it held after the attention moved on, and whether it caused a problem somewhere else.

**Estimated learning time**

Estimate: about 65 minutes (14 minutes of recordings, 50 minutes of exercise).
