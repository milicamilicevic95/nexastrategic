## Module 1: What operations is, and why it is commercial

### Exercise 1: Operations scope map

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Serves outcomes:** M1.1 Explain what operations covers; M1.2 Trace a promise to capacity, margin and cash

**Instructions**

Use the **Operations scope map** resource. You can do this for your own business or, if you prefer not to, for a business you know well.

1. List the three to five customer promises that matter most. Use the ones customers would complain about if broken, not marketing lines.
2. For each, note the main constraint on keeping it: a person, a skill, a supplier, a piece of equipment, time, or cash.
3. List five decisions that come up at least monthly in delivering those promises. Mark the ones that currently need you.
4. List any obligations you know the business has (filings, renewals, licences, insurance, regulated activities). Do not research them now. Just note what you know and what you are unsure about.
5. Pick one promise and answer: who has to do what, what does it cost us to keep, and when does cash go out and come in?

You do not need to share this.

**What the learner produces**

A one-page scope map, with one promise traced through capacity, cost and cash.

**Estimated time**

About 25 minutes.

## Module 3: Why founders become the bottleneck

### Exercise 3: Bottleneck diagnosis

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Serves outcomes:** M3.1 Diagnose bottleneck types with evidence; M3.2 Ask diagnostic questions

**Instructions**

Use the **Bottleneck diagnostic** resource. Work on your own business, or use the practice case below if you prefer not to.

2. For the two strongest, write three diagnostic questions each, using the four question kinds from Lesson 3.2.

## Module 4: Ownership, decisions and when to get advice

### Exercise 4: The 4-hour response promise

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Serves outcomes:** M4.1 Specify owner, decision rights, threshold and escalation; M4.2 Identify consequences of a commitment; M4.3 Recognise when specialist advice is needed

**Instructions**

**Scenario.** Harbour Facilities is a commercial cleaning company with about 40 part-time cleaners and three office staff. The founder, Lena, has just signed a two-year agreement with a property management firm covering 12 office buildings. To win it, she agreed to a 4-hour emergency response, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, for spills, floods and similar incidents. The agreement includes service credits if response times are missed. Payment terms are 60 days. Harbour has no on-call rota. Lena has been answering out-of-hours calls herself. The operations coordinator found out about the 4-hour promise from the client's welcome email.

Using the **Decision rights and thresholds** and **Commitment check and advice brief** resources:

1. **Authority.** Who should have been able to agree a 24/7 response commitment? What approvals should it have needed? Draft three rows of a decision rights table that would have caught this.
2. **Capacity.** What would it take to meet a 4-hour response across 12 sites? What information is missing?
3. **Commercial consequences.** List the effects on margin, cash (60-day terms) and existing clients. Where are the risks of service credits?
4. **Specialist input.** Which questions need a qualified adviser, what kind, and what information would you gather first? Consider the agreement itself and any implications of asking staff to be on call.
5. **Immediate decision.** What should Lena decide this week, and who should be involved?

There is no single right answer. Make your reasoning visible.

**What the learner produces**

Short written answers to the five questions, three rows of a decision rights table and a one-page advice brief.

**Estimated time**

About 45 minutes.

### Setting a threshold: three questions

1. What is the realistic downside if this person gets it wrong? Is it recoverable?
2. How often does this decision come up?
3. Could we check afterwards instead of before?

### Rules of thumb

- One decider per decision.
- Push frequent, low-risk decisions down first.
- Start generous on low-risk decisions and review after a month.
- Every new approval step adds a queue. Remove one for each you add, where you can.
- An owner must be able to do what they own. If not yet, say so and plan for it.

## Module 5: Documentation and SOPs that people use

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Instructions**

### Should this be documented? Decision guide and SOP template

> Status: Draft (not approved)

**Serves outcomes:** M5.1 Decide whether and how to document; M5.2 Distinguish capturing steps from designing a process

**Decision or task it supports**

Decide whether a task needs documenting and in what format, then draft it in a form people can use.

**Resource content**

### Step 1: Does it need documenting?

Document it if **one or more** is true:

- [ ] Frequent (weekly or more)
- [ ] Risky (legal, safety, financial or reputational consequences)
- [ ] Error-prone (keeps going wrong the same way)
- [ ] Depends on one person
- [ ] Rare but must be done right

If none is ticked, it probably does not need a document.

### Step 2: Is the process worth documenting as it is?

- Is there a clear trigger for each step?
- Does it wait for an approval that could be a threshold instead?
- Is anything in it there only because of a one-off past problem?
- Could a step, approval or handoff be removed?
- Is part of the problem a tool, configuration, capacity or ownership issue that a document cannot fix?

Fix what you can **before** writing.

| Set a rule or boundary | Policy line |
| Produce an output with a standard shape | Template |
