Chapter 1 · Map the work, not the people
40:07

Map the work, not the people.

Chapter 1, about four minutes. Before you decide who to hire, you need to see the work.

50:32

Titles last.

When founders draw an org chart, they usually start with boxes and titles. Head of this, manager of that. Then they try to fit people in. It feels like progress, but it skips the only question that matters: what work does the business need done? The order on this slide is the whole method. Map the work first. Then put one owner on each piece. Name the role last, after the work it owns.

70:40

Map the work in four steps

Here’s the method, in four steps. One: list the work in each part of the business, using the eight parts from course 1. Process, people, commercial, finance, legal, compliance, governance, risk. Two: put one name on each. One owner, not a team. If two people own something, nobody does. Three: mark each one. Owned means someone other than you could run it. Only me. Nobody. Or overloaded, where someone owns it but can’t keep up. Four: look at where those marks cluster. That’s where your next hire is, before anyone’s given it a title.

Chapter 2 · When to hire
90:09

When to hire.

Chapter 2, about four minutes. You’ve found the gap. Now, is it time to fill it, and is it really a job?

100:41

Signs it’s time

These are the signs it’s time to hire, on the slide. You’re turning down good work because there’s no capacity. Deadlines or quality are slipping. The same work keeps landing back on your desk. The fourth matters most: you can name the work, and you can count the hours. Vague overwhelm isn’t a job description. If you can’t say what the person would do and how much of it there is, you’re not ready to hire yet. Then, as the line at the bottom says, test it against a slow month. We’ll come to that.

110:36

Count it before you hire for it.

How do you count the hours? Keep a log for two representative weeks. Every time something lands on you that someone else could do, write down four things: the task, how long it took, how often it happens, and the skill it needed. It feels tedious, but it’s the best thirty minutes of admin you’ll do this year. Most founders find the work they thought was a full-time job is two days a week, or the work they thought was small is eating three days.

120:39

Sort what you logged

Then sort what you logged into four piles, down the left. Recurring, core work that happens every week and runs the business: that’s a role. Specialist work you need a few days a month, like finance, legal or design: that’s specialist hours, not a hire. Repeatable work with clear rules: software or a better process. The last pile is the one founders skip. Work that shouldn’t exist at all. Stop doing it. Only the first pile is a hire. If it’s thin, your next hire might not be a person.

130:35

Test the hire against a slow month, not a good one.

One test before any hire. A salary is a fixed cost. It goes out in your slow months too, and most small businesses have slow months. Open your thirteen-week cash view from course 1 and add the hire’s monthly cost to every week. Then look at your lowest week. If that week still holds, you can carry the hire. If it doesn’t, you need a different kind of hire, or a different start date, or the cash to cover the gap first.

Chapter 3 · What kind of hire
140:11

What kind of hire.

Chapter 3, about five minutes. You know the gap and the timing. Now, what kind of hire fills it, and what does it really cost?

150:44

Five ways to fill a gap

There are five ways to fill a gap, and only two of them are an employee. Full-time: core, ongoing work you direct, and the biggest commitment. Part-time: core work that doesn’t fill a week. They have the same rights as full-timers, pro rata, including holiday. Contractor or freelancer: a deliverable, a busy period, or a specialist skill. The watch-out, on the right, is status, which we’ll come to. Fractional or agency: senior judgement for a few days a month. You’re buying experience, not hours. At the bottom, software or a better process. Sometimes the honest answer to who’s your next hire is nobody.

160:59

What a £35,000 hire really costs

Here’s what a thirty-five thousand pound hire really costs, in the UK this tax year. The salary. Employer National Insurance: fifteen percent on everything above five thousand pounds, so four and a half thousand. The minimum employer pension: three percent of qualifying earnings, about eight hundred and sixty. That’s just over forty thousand a year, before equipment, software and your own time getting them started. The Employment Allowance can take up to ten and a half thousand off the business’s total National Insurance bill, though not if your only employee is a director. Then the line at the bottom: with 5.6 weeks of paid holiday, you pay for fifty-two weeks and get about forty-six weeks of work. That isn’t a reason not to hire. It’s a reason to plan for it. Your accountant can confirm your own numbers.

170:54

Calling someone a contractor doesn’t make them one.

One trap with contractors. You can’t choose someone’s employment status by what you call them, or by what the contract says. It follows how the work is actually done. Who controls how, when and where they work? Can they send someone else in their place? Whose equipment do they use? If you set their hours, direct their work and they can’t send a substitute, they may well be an employee, whatever the contract says. HMRC has a free tool, Check Employment Status for Tax, that walks you through it. If you’re a small company and they work through their own limited company, it’s their company that decides their status for tax. If you’re hiring in the US, the rules are different, so check with a US adviser.

180:35

Putting it in order

When you have more than one gap, put them in order. Bottleneck first: fill the gap that costs you the most, in money, missed work, or your time. Doers before managers. A manager needs work and people to manage, and a first manager with nobody to manage ends up doing the work anyway, at a manager’s salary. Titles last. Then the line at the bottom: your next hire is the gap, not the org chart you’d like to have in two years.

190:50

Kestrel’s next moves

Back to Kestrel. The map showed two gaps: delivery, and the founder holding five parts. Delivery is core, weekly work that Marcus directs, so it’s a full-time event producer, an employee. Finance is specialist work Kestrel needs a few days a month, so it’s more bookkeeper days and some fractional finance support, not a finance director. Then payroll, which only Dee can run. That’s a risk, and the fix is training a second person or using a payroll provider. It isn’t a hire at all. That’s the line at the bottom: one hire, one specialist, and one fix that isn’t a hire. Notice there’s no Manchester events lead yet. That decision comes once delivery has capacity.
