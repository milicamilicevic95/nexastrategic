Chapter 1 · When the money arrives
30:09

When the money arrives.

Chapter 1, about three minutes. This is the commercial side: how your prices and your terms decide when the cash turns up.

40:51

Terms in every contract

The commercial panel is where most cash problems start, and these are the four things on this slide. Top one: terms in every contract. Say when you get paid, in writing, every time. If you don’t, the client’s terms win, and theirs are usually longer. Second: a deposit up front, so money comes in before you spend on the work. Kestrel should have had that on day one. Third: invoice the day it’s earned. I see founders sit on invoices for a week or two because they’re busy, and every day you wait is a day added to the gap. At the bottom: one person chases. A name, and a day of the week. Not whoever happens to remember.

50:52

Client A

Look at this. These are Kestrel’s three biggest clients. The numbers are made up, but the pattern isn’t. In the middle, their terms. On the right, when they actually pay. Client B is fine: thirty days, pays in thirty-one. Client A is meant to be thirty and pays in forty-seven. Client C, at the bottom, is sixty days on paper and eighty-five in practice. That’s the line at the bottom: plan on when they pay, not when they should. Your accounting software can usually show you this. If it can’t, take your last few invoices for each client and the date the money landed. It takes about twenty minutes, and it’s one of the most useful things you’ll do this month.

60:45

You’re allowed to charge for it.

One more point on the commercial side. In the UK, if another business pays you late, you’re allowed to charge statutory interest. It’s eight percent plus the Bank of England base rate, and you can add a fixed amount to cover the cost of chasing. That’s as at November 2026, and the government is planning to make the rules on late payment tougher. Most founders never use it, and you don’t have to. Putting it in your terms gives you something to point to when you chase. If a big client is paying you months late, it’s worth knowing the law is on your side.

Chapter 2 · Profit isn’t cash
70:10

Profit isn’t cash.

Chapter 2, about three minutes. This is the finance side: why profit and cash are different, and the one tool that shows you both.

80:41

On paper

Here’s Kestrel in March. Remember the big contract from module 1? Top row, on paper. They invoiced a hundred and twenty thousand and spent ninety-five on venues, freelancers and payroll. That’s twenty-five thousand profit. Great month. Bottom row, in the bank. The ninety-five thousand went out in March. The hundred and twenty comes in May, because of those ninety-day terms. So, as it says at the bottom: profitable in March, short of cash until May. Your profit and loss would tell you March was a great month. Only a cash view tells you it nearly wasn’t.

90:48

Start

This is the tool: a thirteen-week cash view. It’s the standard way finance people look at cash, and it’s simple to run. You start with your bank balance today, top row. Then money in: each invoice, in the week it’ll actually land. The week it lands, rather than the week it’s due, which is why we just looked at clients A, B and C. Then money out: payroll, rent, suppliers, tax, in the week each one goes. At the bottom, every week, you roll it forward one week. It takes about fifteen minutes once it’s set up. There’s a template with the worksheet, so you’re not starting from a blank spreadsheet.

100:28

You see a cash gap weeks before it hits.

This is why you do it. You see a cash gap weeks before it hits, which means you can do something about it. Chase earlier, ask for a deposit, move a payment, talk to the bank, hold off a hire. Find out on the day payroll’s due, and you’ve got none of those options. Find out eight weeks ahead, and you’ve got all of them.

110:49

VAT

Tax next, because it’s the money-out line founders forget. This is UK, as at November 2026, and it’s here so you recognise it. VAT, top row: you have to register once your taxable turnover passes ninety thousand pounds in any twelve months. Corporation tax: for most companies, it’s due nine months and a day after your year end. Larger companies pay in instalments. PAYE goes every month with payroll. The bottom one is the habit: set tax aside as the money comes in, in a separate account, so it’s never sitting in your cash looking like it’s yours. Your accountant should confirm exactly what applies to you, and put the dates in your cash view.

Chapter 3 · Who does the numbers
120:08

Who does the numbers.

Chapter 3, about two minutes. Who should be doing all this, and when you need a finance hire.

130:49

Bookkeeper

Who does all of this? This is the ladder, from the top. A bookkeeper keeps the records, sends invoices and reconciles the bank, every week. An accountant handles year end, tax, and advice when you ask. A part-time finance director covers forecasts, planning and fundraising, usually when you’re raising or planning something big. A CFO, at the bottom, is finance strategy at scale, and usually much later than founders think. The line underneath is the one to hold on to: most businesses your size need the first two, done well. Not a CFO. If your bookkeeping is a month behind, no finance director in the world can tell you what your cash looks like.

140:51

Add one what-if.

One last Kestrel point. When they took the big contract, they hired three freelancers straight away, before the first payment had landed. The hiring outran the money. A thirteen-week view with one what-if in it would have shown them that. So that’s the habit on this slide: add one what-if to your cash view. The one I always ask is underneath: what if your biggest client pays a month late? Move their money four weeks to the right and look at your lowest week. If that week goes below zero, you know what to fix first. Notice how this turns the ball, too. One late payment hits finance, but also people, because of payroll, and risk, because it’s one client.
