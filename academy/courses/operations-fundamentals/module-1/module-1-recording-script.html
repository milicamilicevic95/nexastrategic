Chapter 1 · What operations is
40:11

What operations is.

Chapter 1. This is about five minutes, and it answers one question: what is operations? Most founders I work with have never had anyone explain it.

50:30

What is operations?

This is the question I ask every founder I work with. Have a go before I answer. Pause it if you want. ▸ Pause. Most people say some version of process. Systems, SOPs, admin, getting things written down. That’s not wrong. It’s just one part of it. Process is one of eight, and I’ll show you the other seven in a minute.

60:35

Everything that makes the business work, behind what the customer sees.

This is the definition I use, and it’s the one we’ll stick with for the whole course. Operations is everything that makes the business work, behind what the customer sees. The customer sees your product, your service, your marketing, you. Everything behind that, from delivering to getting paid, staying legal and keeping going, is operations. When it works, nobody notices. When it doesn’t, the customer notices quickly. An invoice goes out wrong, a deadline gets missed, a contract says something nobody expected.

70:18

You’ve been doing it since your first invoice.

You already have operations, even if it’s just you. The first time you agreed a price, sent an invoice or signed a contract, that was operations. Nobody called it that, so nobody owns it. Which usually means it all lives in your head.

80:52

The work

Here are the eight. I call them panels, and you’ll see why in chapter 2. Eight’s a lot to hold, so I put them in four pairs, which is what you’re looking at. Top row, the work: process and people. How the work gets done, and who does it. Then the money: commercial and finance. What you charge, and what actually comes in and goes out. Then the rules: legal and compliance. What you’ve agreed to, and what you have to follow. The bottom row is control: governance and risk. Who decides, and what could go wrong. Work, money, rules, control. Remember those four and you’ll remember the eight. ▸ If you’re on paper: draw four boxes and put two words in each.

91:18

Process

I’m not going to define all eight for you. You know what finance is, and the prompts are on your worksheet. What I want to show you instead is on this slide: the gap I look for first in each one. Process, the top one. It works, but only because one person remembers how. People: everyone’s clear on who does what, until someone leaves. Commercial: discounts agreed on a call and never written down. Finance: profit isn’t cash. You can be profitable and still not make payroll. We’ll cover that properly in module 3. Legal: who actually owns your templates, your code, your content. Compliance: you’re following the rules, but you couldn’t show it if someone asked. Governance: nobody’s quite sure who’s allowed to say yes. Risk, at the bottom: the one client, supplier or person you can’t afford to lose. Look down that list and notice which one made you wince. One more thing to hold on to: think of one decision you made in the last month. A client, a hire, a price, a supplier. You’ll use it in the exercise.

Chapter 2 · One ball
100:10

One ball.

Chapter 2, about four minutes. This is why the eight only work as one thing, and who in your business is holding it all.

110:39

Eight panels. One ball.

This is why I call them panels. Think of a beach ball. Eight panels sewn together: that’s operations. You can look at one panel at a time, but you can’t take one off and still have a ball. Every decision you make lands on the whole thing. A new client is a commercial decision, but it’s also a finance one, a people one, a legal one. You might only be looking at one colour when you decide. ▸ On paper: draw a circle, split it into eight, and write a name in each.

120:48

Things fall through the seams.

As companies grow, they tend to cut the ball up. Legal ops, finance ops, rev ops, people ops. Each team gets very good at its own panel, which is fine. The problem is that each team only sees its own colour. Finance sees the payment terms. Legal sees the contract. Nobody sees that the payment terms, the contract and the staffing plan are all the same decision. Split the ball, and you split who does the work, who answers for it, and who knows how it works. In my experience, problems don’t sit on a panel. They sit in the gaps between them. That’s what the slide says: things fall through the seams.

131:36

One form. Four teams. Nobody told.

Let me give you a real one. I worked at a regulated financial services scale-up, a couple of hundred people. After they changed regulator, the compliance team rewrote the client onboarding form. New requirements, extra paperwork for some long-standing clients. A reasonable change. But they launched it on their own. The account managers didn’t know, so they couldn’t warn their clients. The ops team doing the onboarding didn’t know. Sales didn’t know. When I saw it, I flagged it. I said we need a change log, we need everyone affected in a room, and we need to tell clients before anything changes. I told my manager and the chief compliance officer we were going to lose clients over this. And we did. Established clients, with significant money with us, who didn’t want to redo paperwork nobody had told them about. Afterwards, changes started going round the teams for input before they launched. It tightened up properly once onboarding had its own team who owned the process. Until then, the onboarding team was me. I was onboarding and offboarding everyone, and spotting problems that were never my job to spot. Yet nobody got their own bit wrong. Compliance did compliance right. It went wrong in the gap between compliance, the commercial side and process. ▸ Look up. What fixed it in the end was an owner.

140:28

You’re holding the ball. Because nobody decided otherwise.

In most founder-led businesses, the founder is holding the ball. Nobody actually decided that. You set the first prices, signed the first contracts, hired the first people, and it all stayed with you. That works, for a while. It stops working when you’re the only person who can answer a question about any of the eight, and you’re also meant to be running the business.

150:55

1

This slide shows roughly how that changes as you grow. Step one, top of the list: it’s just you, holding every panel. Then panels get helpers. A bookkeeper, a freelancer, an assistant. They do the work, but you still make the calls. Then panels get owners, people who make decisions within limits you’ve agreed. That’s the shift David talks about: handing over decisions, not just tasks. Eventually, someone who isn’t you holds the whole ball, usually an ops lead or a COO. As it says at the bottom, it’s a common pattern, not a rule, and not every business needs that last step. What matters is being honest about which of your panels have owners, and which are still just you. They’re rarely all at the same stage.

160:36

You can hand over the work. The business keeps the responsibility.

This is the one I most want you to remember. You can hand over the work. You can’t hand over the responsibility. If your bookkeeper files late, the company’s late. If a freelancer mishandles customer data, it’s your business that has to answer for it. So hand panels over, by all means. But you still need to know who has it, and how you’d know if it was going wrong. That’s the last column on your map: how would you know it’s slipping?

Chapter 3 · Turn the ball
170:09

Turn the ball.

Chapter 3, about four minutes. This is where we take one real decision and turn it all the way round.

181:08

Finance

Back to Kestrel. From the commercial panel, it’s the best deal they’ve ever had. Now let’s turn it. I’ve put each panel down the left. Finance: the client pays in 90 days, but venues want deposits up front. In effect, Kestrel is funding the client’s events for three months. People: the team’s already full, so they’ll need freelancers, which means contracts and onboarding. Process: the client wants to sign off every single event, which adds a step to everything. Legal: the contract gives the client all the creative work, including templates Kestrel has used for years. Compliance: attendee personal data would end up on staff’s personal drives. Governance: the founder agreed it alone, on a call, and nobody else knew. And risk, at the bottom: this one client would be half their revenue. That’s what the line at the bottom of the slide is saying. Same deal, seven more things to know. So, would you still sign it?

190:43

Say yes knowingly.

To be clear, the point isn’t to say no. It might still be a great deal. The point is to say yes knowingly, and change what you can, which is the line on screen. Push for better payment terms, or a deposit. Get your templates taken out of the IP clause. Move the data somewhere it should be. Agree who signs off on deals this size. And decide, properly, whether you can live with one client being half the business. One caveat: the legal and data points here are examples, not legal advice. If it’s a real contract, get it checked.
