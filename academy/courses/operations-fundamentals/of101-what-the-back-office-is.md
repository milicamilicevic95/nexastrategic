## Why this module exists

Most founders never learn what the back office is. They start a business to make or sell something, and the work that keeps it paid, staffed, legal and organised arrives in pieces: an invoice to send, a contract to sign, an insurance renewal, a new starter who needs a laptop. Because nobody names it, nobody owns it, and it ends up in the founder's head and inbox. This module gives it a name, a shape and a first map.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of this module, the founder can:

- **OF101.1** Name the four areas of the back office and give two examples of the work in each.
- **OF101.2** Map who does each area in their business today, where it lives, and where the gaps are.

Evidence: the back office map (exercise).

## Principle, method, tactic

**Principle.** Every business has a back office from day one, whether or not anyone runs it. Each part needs an owner.

**Method.** Map the four areas (money, people, company, systems): what work each involves, who does it now, where it lives, and how confident you are that it's done.

**Tactic** *(may be updated)*:
1. List the work you know happens in each of the four areas.
2. Note who does each piece now, including people outside the business.
3. Note where it lives: an app, a folder, an inbox, or your head.
4. Mark each one: done and owned, done but only by you, or unsure.
5. Circle the three that worry you most.

## Lesson 1.1 · What the back office is (about 6 minutes)

**Summary.** The difference between the front office and the back office, and the four areas every back office has.

**Script**

[ON SCREEN: "What is the back office?"]

If you've never worked in operations, nobody has ever told you what the back office is. So let's start there.

Every business has two sides.

[ON SCREEN: front office / back office]

The **front office** is everything the customer sees. Marketing, sales, the product or service itself, customer conversations. It's the part you probably started the business to do.

The **back office** is everything that keeps the business running behind that. The customer never sees it. But if it stops, they feel it quickly. An invoice goes out wrong. Someone isn't paid on time. A contract gets signed that nobody can find. A renewal is missed.

You'll also hear the word **operations**. People use it in different ways. In this course, operations means how the business actually runs, day to day. The back office is the part of operations that keeps the business itself working. That's what this course teaches.

[ON SCREEN: "Every business has a back office from day one."]

Here's the first thing to understand. You have a back office already. Even if it's just you. The day you send your first invoice, you've started a finance function. The day you hire your first person, you've started a people function. The question isn't whether you have a back office. It's whether anyone is running it.

[ON SCREEN: the four areas: Money, People, Company, Systems]

The back office has four areas. Learn these four words, because the rest of the course is built on them.

**Money.** Keeping the business paid. Sending invoices and getting paid. Paying suppliers. Recording every transaction, which is called bookkeeping. Paying your people. Setting money aside for tax. And knowing how much cash you actually have.

**People.** Keeping the business staffed. The paperwork when you hire someone. Their contract. Paying them. Getting them started. Holidays, sickness and other leave. What happens when they leave. And keeping proper records of all of it.

**Company.** Keeping the business legal. The company's own records and the things it has to file. Registering for the right taxes. Insurance. Contracts with customers and suppliers, and when they renew. Looking after people's personal data. And any licences your sector needs.

**Systems.** Keeping the business organised. Your email and domain. Where files live. Who has passwords and access to what. The tools you run on. And writing down how things are done, so it isn't all in one person's head.

[ON SCREEN: "Paid. Staffed. Legal. Organised."]

Paid, staffed, legal, organised. That's the job of the back office.

[ON SCREEN: what it isn't]

Two things the back office is not.

It's not "just admin". Some of it is admin. But a missed tax deadline, an unclear contract or a payroll error can cost real money and real trust.

And it's not something you sort out once you're bigger. The habits you set with three people are the ones you'll have with thirty, unless you change them on purpose.

[ON SCREEN: prompt]

Before the next lesson, take a minute. For each of the four areas, think of one thing that happened in your business last month. An invoice. A new starter. A renewal. A password someone needed. We'll use them next.

**Slides:** front and back office; four areas; paid, staffed, legal, organised; what it isn't; prompt.

## Lesson 1.2 · How the back office grows with you (about 6 minutes)

**Summary.** Who does the back office at each stage of a founder-led business, and why it ends up in the founder's head.

**Script**

[ON SCREEN: "Who does the back office?"]

In the last lesson you thought of one thing from each area. Now the useful question: who did it?

In most founder-led businesses, the answer is the founder. Not because it's a good idea. Because nobody decided otherwise.

[ON SCREEN: three stages]

Here's how the back office usually grows. This is a typical pattern, not a rule, and your business may move faster or slower.

[ON SCREEN: Just you, and a few others]

**Stage one: you, and maybe a few others.** The founder does nearly everything. An external accountant handles the year-end accounts and tax returns. Maybe a bookkeeper a few hours a month. Everything else lives in the founder's head, their inbox and their banking app. That's fine at this size, as long as the founder knows what the four areas need.

[ON SCREEN: The first hires]

**Stage two: the first hires.** Once you employ people, the people area arrives properly: contracts, payroll, onboarding, leave. Often a payroll provider is brought in. Recurring work starts to need writing down, because you can no longer just remember it. This is when many businesses hire an office manager, an operations assistant or an executive assistant, someone to hold the back office together day to day.

[ON SCREEN: A growing team]

**Stage three: a growing team.** The areas start to separate. Someone looks after finance. Someone looks after people. Managers appear. The founder can no longer check everything, so they need to see what's happening through simple reports instead. This is often when a business brings in a head of operations, a COO, or a fractional one, someone senior who works part-time across several businesses.

[ON SCREEN: "The work stays the same. Who does it changes."]

Notice what changes and what doesn't. The four areas are the same at every stage. Paid, staffed, legal, organised. What changes is who does the work, how much of it is written down, and how much the founder has to hold personally.

[ON SCREEN: "Outsourcing the work isn't outsourcing the responsibility."]

One more thing that catches founders out. You can pay someone outside the business to do back-office work: an accountant, a payroll provider, an HR adviser. That's often the right call. But they do what you ask them to do, with the information you give them. The business is still responsible. So you still need to know what's being done, by whom, and what they're relying on you for.

[ON SCREEN: recap]

So, to recap. The back office is the work that keeps the business paid, staffed, legal and organised. It has four areas: money, people, company and systems. You have one from day one. And at every stage, the question is the same: who owns each part, and does anyone other than you know how it's done?

The exercise for this module asks you to map that for your business. It takes about 25 minutes. You don't need to share it with anyone.

**Slides:** who did it; three stages; same work, different people; outsourcing isn't responsibility; recap.

## Worked example

*Fictional. Used in the deck.*

**Priya runs a seven-person design studio.** She's a brilliant designer and has never worked in operations. Here's her back office, area by area:

- **Money.** Priya sends invoices from a template on her laptop, when she remembers. She checks the bank app to see who's paid. The accountant does the year-end accounts.
- **People.** The contracts came from a template a friend shared. Payroll is run by the accountant from an email Priya sends each month. New starters are onboarded by Priya, differently each time.
- **Company.** Priya isn't sure when the insurance renews. Client contracts are in her email. The accountant "does the filings".
- **Systems.** Files are split across personal drives. Priya holds most of the passwords. Nothing is written down.

Nothing here is broken yet. But every area runs through Priya, and most of it lives in her head. If she's away for two weeks, invoices stop, the payroll email doesn't get sent, and nobody can find a contract.

The point isn't that Priya is disorganised. It's that nobody ever told her the back office was a thing, so nobody owns it.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The back office is for when we're bigger."** You have one from your first invoice. The question is whether anyone runs it.
- **"My accountant does all that."** Your accountant does what you've engaged them to do. The rest of the money area, and the other three areas, still need an owner.
- **"It's just admin."** Mistakes here cost money, trust and sometimes fines.
- **"We need software first."** A tool helps once you know what the work is and who owns it. Before that, it just moves the mess somewhere new.

## Exercise 1 · Your back office map (about 25 minutes)

Use the **Back office map** template. Do it for your own business, or a business you know well if you'd rather not.

1. For each of the four areas, list the work you know happens. Use the examples in the template as prompts; cross out anything that doesn't apply.
2. For each piece of work, note who does it now. Include people outside the business, such as your accountant or payroll provider.
3. Note where it lives: an app, a folder, an inbox, or someone's head.
4. Mark each one: **owned** (done, and someone other than you could explain it), **only me** (done, but only you know how), or **unsure** (you don't know if it's done, or how).
5. Circle the three that worry you most.

You don't need to share this. You'll use it in every module that follows.

**Output:** a one-page map of your back office, with the gaps marked.
