Chapter 1 · What you’ve signed
40:11

What you’ve signed.

Chapter 1, about two minutes. This is the legal panel: the documents that matter at your size, and what to look for when you read them.

51:03

How long, and renewal

At your size, there are usually three documents that really matter. Your customer terms or contracts, your biggest supplier contracts, and your shareholders’ agreement and articles if you have investors. And when you read any of them, these are the five things on the slide to find. Top one: how long is it, and does it renew on its own? If so, what’s the notice period? Payment: when you pay or get paid, which we did in module 3. Who owns the work, so your templates, your code, your content. Remember Kestrel’s IP clause from module 1. Liability: how much could you owe if it goes wrong. And at the bottom: how do you get out, and what does ending it early cost? You don’t have to understand every clause. You have to find those five, and if one looks wrong, that’s your question for a lawyer.

60:35

One clause nobody had read.

Here’s Kestrel. Years ago, they signed up for event booking software, on a one-year contract. Nobody read the renewal clause. It renewed automatically for three years, unless they gave ninety days’ notice. Nobody had the date. So by the time they wanted to switch, they were locked in for another three years. Nothing illegal, nobody did anything wrong on paper. It was just one clause nobody had read, and one date nobody owned. Which is exactly what the next chapter fixes.

Chapter 2 · What’s due
70:07

What’s due.

Chapter 2, about two minutes. This is compliance: the deadlines that actually bite, all in one place.

81:08

Companies House

So this is the fix. One calendar, every deadline, with an owner on each. These are the five groups on the slide, UK, as at November 2026. Companies House, top: your confirmation statement at least once a year, your accounts nine months after your year end, and directors now have to verify their identity with Companies House, which is tied to that confirmation statement. HMRC: the VAT, corporation tax and payroll dates from module 3. Data protection: most businesses that use personal data have to pay a fee to the ICO, unless they’re exempt, and a breach that puts people at risk has to be reported within seventy-two hours. Which is exactly where my story from the start came in. Insurance: every renewal date, including employers’ liability from module 2. And contracts, at the bottom: the renewal and notice dates you just found. Every one of those gets a date and a name. Not the team. A name.

Chapter 3 · Who decides, and what could go wrong
90:09

Who decides, and what could go wrong.

Chapter 3, about three minutes. Governance and risk. Who can say yes to what, and your biggest risks, each with an owner.

100:56

Discounts

Governance sounds like boards and committees, but at your size it’s mostly this slide. Who can say yes to what, and up to how much. This is Kestrel’s, and the numbers are theirs. Down the left, the decision. In the middle, who decides. On the right, the limit. Discounts: the account manager, up to ten percent. Spending: the ops lead, up to two thousand pounds. Freelancers: the events lead, within the event budget. And contracts: anything over twelve months or twenty thousand pounds goes to the founder. Remember that renewal clause? That’s the line that would have caught it. And this is usually where delegation fails, the thing we talked about in module 2. The task moves, but the decision never does. Writing this down is how the decision actually moves.

110:58

One client is half the revenue

And then risk. Your five biggest, each with a name. Not a fifty-line register, just five. These are Kestrel’s, and you’ll recognise a few from module 1. Down the left, the risk. In the middle, the owner. On the right, what they’d do. One client is half the revenue: the founder owns that, and the plan is to win two more this year. Only one person can run payroll: the ops lead gets it written down and trains a second person. Attendee data on personal drives: the account lead moves it to one system. A venue cancels late: the events lead keeps back-up venues and checks the cover in the contracts. And a key person leaves: that’s handovers, which is module 2. So five risks, five names, and one thing each of them would actually do.

So when do you actually call someone? These are the four on the slide. One: it’s a contract you can’t easily walk away from. Long, expensive, or with your IP in it. Two: people could be harmed, or their data could be exposed. Three: it’s over your own limit, the one you just wrote down for everyone else. And four: you genuinely can’t work out what the question is. And then the line at the bottom, which goes back to my story: bring one precise question. What happened, what you’ve already done, and exactly what you need to know. You’ll get a faster answer, and usually a smaller bill.
