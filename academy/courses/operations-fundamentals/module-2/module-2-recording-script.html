Chapter 1 · Get it out of your head
30:09

Get it out of your head.

Chapter 1, about three minutes. How the work really gets done, and how to write it down so it’s actually used.

41:05

Trigger

Before you write anything, map one process. Three things. The trigger: the event that starts it, like a client signing off, rather than ‘monthly’ or ‘when needed’. The handoffs: every point the work changes hands, because that’s where it gets dropped. And the record: the proof it happened. Take Kestrel, our events agency from module 1. Invoicing starts when the client signs off an event. The account manager passes it to the founder, the founder raises the invoice, it goes to the client, and the record is the invoice in the client folder. Look at the middle. The founder. Every handoff that goes through you is where the work waits, because you’re doing ten other things. Map yours and circle every handoff that goes through you. That’s your list. ▸ On paper: trigger on the left, record on the right, an arrow for every handoff, and circle the ones that go through you.

51:06

Steps last.

When founders write a process down, they start with the steps. Log in, click here, fill this in. That’s why most SOPs get ignored. Steps are the part anyone could copy. Start with why it exists. That’s the line that makes people follow it when nobody’s checking. For Kestrel: we pay venues up front, so every day an invoice is late, we’re funding the client. Then say what they can decide alone, and when they stop and ask. If you hand over the steps and keep the decisions, the work comes back to you. Only then, the steps, and only the ones a capable new person would get wrong. There are nine questions in total; they’re on your worksheet and in the SOP guide, with a weak and a strong answer for each. One rule for all of them: if you can answer it without thinking about your own business, you haven’t answered it yet.

60:45

Give it to someone who’s never done it.

Then test it. Give it to someone capable who has never done the task, and watch them do it once without helping. That last bit is the hard part. Every question they ask is a gap. Every step they skip needs a reason written next to it. Every place they hesitate needs a name, a number or a link. In a small team there may be no stranger, and the person you’re handing it to will do. If it’s a live client or money task, they do it for real and you check it before it goes out. Fix the gaps and test again.

Chapter 2 · Hand it over
70:10

Hand it over.

Chapter 2, about three minutes. Writing it down isn’t the same as handing it over. This is how you make it stay handed over.

81:02

1

Four steps, in order. One: show them one real example, and think out loud while you do it. Say why you check what you check. They’re learning how you think. Two: they teach it back. They do the next one while you watch, and you ask what they’d change, what they’d test first, and where they still need you. Three: split who checks from who fixes, and agree what stops it going out. Otherwise they spot a problem, can’t fix it, and it lands back on your desk. Four: hand over the decision, with a limit. This is David’s point: hand over the decisions, as well as the tasks. If the decision stays with you, the work comes back to you. The handover kit walks you through all four. Think about the last thing you handed over that came back. Which step did it skip?

90:34

“Use your judgement, but run anything big past me.”

Here’s step four with Kestrel. The founder hands invoicing to an account manager. The weak version: use your judgement, but run anything big past me. What counts as big? Nobody knows, so everything comes back. The strong version: you send invoices up to ten thousand pounds that are within five percent of the contract, on your own. Anything else, or if the client disputes the sign-off, you ask. Now they know exactly what’s theirs. The numbers are Kestrel’s. Pick yours.

101:00

Four weeks off your plate.

A handover counts once it has stayed off your plate for four weeks. Explaining it doesn’t count. But off your plate can also mean quietly going wrong, and late invoices show up in your cash weeks later. So pick one day a week and look at the record: the tracker, the folder, whatever proves it happened. Check the results, and never redo the work. Watch for the signs it’s coming back: they ask permission for things inside their limit, you quietly re-check their work, or you fix things yourself instead of sending them back. If it comes back, don’t take it back. Ask which step it skipped, and redo that one. And notice that this one handover also protected Kestrel’s cash and removed a single point of failure. After four weeks, hand over the next thing. One at a time.

Chapter 3 · The people basics
110:08

The people basics.

Chapter 3, about three minutes. While you’re writing things down, check the basics for everyone who works for you.

120:42

“Fill as needed.”

▸ Look up. I joined a scale-up early, and it grew very fast. Investors wanted growth, and the whole plan rested on one big approval coming through, with no plan B. Teams were given budgets and told, fill as needed. So they filled seats. When the approval didn’t come, there wasn’t enough work for the people we’d hired. By the time I was made redundant, there had been two rounds of redundancies. From where I sat, nobody was asked to look at the whole. A role should exist because there’s work for it, and that work should be written down.

131:30

A written contract

Five basics for everyone who works for you. This is the UK, as at November 2026, and I’m keeping it to recognising them. One: a written contract. Employees and workers get a written statement of their main terms on day one, and the rest within two months. Two: right to work. You check it before they start and record the date. The penalty is up to forty-five thousand pounds per worker for a first breach, and since October the rules also cover some individual sub-contractors. Three: payroll and pension. Employees are paid through payroll and reported to HMRC, and eligible staff are enrolled in a workplace pension that you pay into. Four: employers’ liability insurance. You need it from the day you become an employer, unless your only staff are close family. Five: a clear role. That one isn’t law. It’s one line saying what they own, so two people don’t end up doing the same job. One change to know: from January 2027, employees can claim unfair dismissal after six months in the job, instead of two years. So contracts, probation and clear roles matter from the start. In the US it’s different. If you’re unsure on any of this, ask an employment adviser; module 4 covers when.

140:28

If it isn’t written down, it’s a memory.

That’s the point of all five, and of the SOP. If it isn’t written down, it’s a memory, and two people will remember it differently. A verbal agreement can still count, so this is about having something you can point to. Think back to the story: budgets, seats, and no written reason for the roles. When things got hard, there was nothing to point to.
