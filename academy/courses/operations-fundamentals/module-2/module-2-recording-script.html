Chapter 1 · Get it out of your head
30:10

Get it out of your head.

Chapter 1, about three minutes. This is how the work really gets done, and how to write it down so people use it.

41:11

Trigger

Before you write anything, map it. There are three things to find, down the left of this slide. The trigger, top row: the event that starts it, like a client signing off, rather than ‘monthly’ or ‘when needed’. Handoffs, the middle: every point the work changes hands, because that’s where it gets dropped. The record, at the bottom: the proof it happened. On the right I’ve done it for Kestrel, our events agency from module 1. Invoicing starts when the client signs off an event. The account manager passes it to the founder, the founder raises the invoice, and it goes to the client. Look at the middle. The founder. That’s the line at the bottom: every handoff that goes through you is where it waits, because you’re doing ten other things. When you map yours, circle every handoff that goes through you. That’s your list. ▸ On paper: trigger on the left, record on the right, an arrow for every handoff, circle the ones through you.

51:00

Steps last.

When founders write a process down, they start with the steps. Log in, click here, fill this in. That’s why most SOPs get ignored. Steps are the bit anyone could copy. Put them last. Look at the order on the slide. First: why does it exist? That’s what makes people follow it when nobody’s checking. For Kestrel it’s: we pay venues up front, so every day an invoice is late, we’re funding the client. Then: what can they decide on their own, and when do they stop and ask? Only then, last, the steps, and only the ones a capable new person would get wrong. There are nine questions in all, on the worksheet and in the SOP guide. One rule for all of them: if you could answer it without thinking about your own business, you haven’t really answered it.

60:45

Give it to someone who’s never done it.

Then test it. This is the stranger test. Give it to someone capable who’s never done it, and watch them do it once without helping. That’s the hard part. Every question they ask is a gap. Every step they skip needs a reason written next to it. Every time they hesitate, it needs a name, a number or a link. If you’re a small team and there’s no stranger, the person you’re handing it to will do. If it’s a live client or money task, they do it for real and you check it before it goes out. Fix the gaps, then test it again.

Chapter 2 · Hand it over
70:11

Hand it over.

Chapter 2, about three minutes. Writing it down isn’t the same as handing it over, so this is how you make it stay handed over.

81:14

1

Four steps, on the slide in order. On the left, the step. On the right, what it stops. One: show them one real example, and think out loud while you do it. Say why you check what you check, so they learn how you think. Two: they teach it back. They do the next one while you watch, and you ask what they’d change, what they’d test first, and where they still need you. That’s how you find the gaps before they cost anything. Three: split who checks from who fixes, and agree what stops it going out. Otherwise they spot a problem, can’t fix it, and it lands back on your desk. Four, at the bottom: hand over the decision, with a limit. This is David’s point: hand over the decisions as well as the tasks. If the decision stays with you, the work comes back to you. The handover kit takes you through all four. Think about the last thing you handed over that came back. Which step did it skip?

90:36

“Use your judgement, but run anything big past me.”

Here’s step four with Kestrel. The founder’s handing invoicing to an account manager. On the left, the weak version: use your judgement, but run anything big past me. What counts as big? Nobody knows, so everything comes back. On the right, the strong one: you send invoices up to ten thousand pounds that are within five per cent of the contract, on your own. Anything else, you ask. Now they know exactly what’s theirs. The numbers are Kestrel’s, by the way. Pick your own.

101:03

Four weeks off your plate.

When does a handover count? When it’s stayed off your plate for four weeks. Explaining it doesn’t count. But off your plate can also mean quietly going wrong, and late invoices turn up in your cash weeks later. Hence the line under it: check the record each week. Pick a day and look at the tracker or the folder, whatever proves it happened. Check the results, and never redo the work. Watch for the signs it’s coming back. They ask permission for things inside their limit. You quietly re-check their work. You fix things yourself instead of sending them back. If it does come back, don’t take it back. Ask which step it skipped, and redo that one. Notice that this one handover also protected Kestrel’s cash and removed a single point of failure. After four weeks, hand over the next thing. One at a time.

Chapter 3 · The people basics
110:09

The people basics.

Chapter 3, about three minutes. While you’re writing things down anyway, this is the basics for everyone who works for you.

120:48

“Fill as needed.”

▸ Look up. Let me tell you what I saw. I joined a scale-up early, and it grew fast. Investors wanted growth, and the whole plan rested on one big approval coming through, with no plan B. Teams were given budgets and told, and this is the quote on the slide, fill as needed. So they filled seats. When the approval didn’t come, there wasn’t enough work for the people we’d hired. By the time I was made redundant, there’d been two rounds of redundancies. From where I sat, nobody was asked to look at the whole. A role should exist because there’s work for it, and that work should be written down.

131:29

A written contract

Five basics for everyone who works for you, down the left. This is the UK, as at November 2026, and I’ll keep it to recognising them. Top one, a written contract. Employees and workers get a written statement of their main terms on day one, and the rest within two months. Right to work: you check it before they start, and record the date. The penalty is up to forty-five thousand pounds per worker for a first breach, and since October it also covers some individual sub-contractors. Payroll and pension: paid through payroll, reported to HMRC, and eligible staff enrolled in a workplace pension that you pay into. Liability insurance: employers’ liability, from the day you become an employer, unless your only staff are close family. And a clear role, at the bottom, which isn’t law, as it says. It’s one line saying what they own, so two people don’t end up doing the same job. One change to know about: from January 2027, employees can claim unfair dismissal after six months, instead of two years. That’s why contracts, probation and clear roles matter from the start. In the US it’s different. If you’re unsure on any of this, ask an employment adviser. Module 4 covers when.

140:27

If it isn’t written down, it’s a memory.

That’s the point of all five, and of the SOP. If it isn’t written down, it’s a memory. Two people will remember it differently. A verbal agreement can still count, so this is about having something you can point to. Think back to the story: budgets, seats, and no written reason for the roles. When it got hard, there was nothing to point to.
