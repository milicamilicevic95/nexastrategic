The handover kit.

How to hand work over so it doesn’t come back to you.

Why handovers come back

1 · The task left, the decision didn’t

They can do the work, but every judgement call still comes to you.

2 · Nobody checked they could

You explained it once and hoped. The first mistake sends it straight back.

3 · Checking and fixing got blurred

They spot a problem but can’t fix it, so it lands back on your desk.

The method: four steps, in order

It’s done when

it has stayed off your plate for four weeks. Then hand over the next thing. One at a time.

Steps 1 and 2

Show it once. Then have them show you.

1

Show one real example, thinking out loud

Use a live piece of work, not a hypothetical one. As you go, say why you make each choice: what you check first, what you ignore, what would make you stop. They’re learning your judgement, not your clicks.

  • Pick an example with at least one decision in it.
  • Say out loud the things you’d normally do without thinking.
  • Have them write it up, not you. Their notes become the SOP draft.
2

They teach it back

They do the next one while you watch, explaining as they go. Then ask three questions:

What would you change?

Shows whether they understand why it’s done this way.

What would you test first?

Shows whether they know where it breaks.

Where do you still need me?

Shows you what hasn’t been handed over yet.

If they can’t explain a step in their own words, explain it again a different way, then ask again. Healthcare calls this “teach-back” and uses it to check understanding. It takes minutes, and it shows you what’s unclear before you write more instructions.

Weak“Does that make sense?” “Yes.”
Strong“Talk me through the next one. What would you change, and where do you need me?”

Watch for

  • Doing it for them when they’re slow the first time. Slow is the price of it never coming back.
  • Handing over a broken process. Fix it, or say plainly what’s broken, before it becomes their problem.
  • No access. They can’t own what they can’t log in to.

Steps 3 and 4

Split the check from the fix. Then hand over the decision.

3

Who checks, who fixes, who decides

Someone can own checking the work without owning the fix, as long as they have the access and training to check it properly. Write it down.

WhoExample: a campaign launch (Kestrel, fictional)
Does itThe person doing the workJo builds the campaign.
Checks itOwns confirming it works before it goes outJo checks the whole journey: offer, page and follow-up messages match; sends a test lead; confirms it lands in the right place and gets the right messages.
Fixes itOwns repairing what the check findsThe founder fixes the backend, for now, in time blocked out for it.
DecidesSays whether it goes outJo, unless an essential check fails.
ToldNeeds to know, doesn’t actSales lead, on launch day.

The rule that makes it work: nothing launches while an essential check is failing. Agree in advance which checks are essential.

4

Hand over the decision, with a limit

If the decision stays with you, the work comes back to you. Say what they can decide alone, up to what limit, and what makes them stop.

Weak“Use your judgement, but run anything big past me.”
Strong“You decide launch timing and copy changes. Stop and ask if spend goes over £[X], or if an essential check fails.”

Signs it’s coming back

  • They ask permission for things inside their limit.
  • You quietly re-check their work.
  • You fix things yourself rather than send them back.
  • Problems reach you before they reach them.

If it comes back

Don’t take it back. Ask which step failed (they hadn’t seen it, couldn’t do it, couldn’t fix it, or weren’t allowed to decide), and redo that step only.
