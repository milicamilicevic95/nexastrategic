How to use it

  1. Pick one process that keeps going wrong, or that only one person knows.
  2. Read pages 2 to 6. It takes about fifteen minutes.
  3. Have the person who does the work fill in the template with you. Not you alone.
  4. Give it to someone who has never done the task. Fix every place they got stuck.
  5. Put a review date on it, and keep it where the work happens.
  6. Using Claude or ChatGPT? Read page 8 first: it’s an interviewer, not an author.
  7. One at a time. It’s locked in when the task has stayed off your plate for four weeks. Then start the next.

Before you write one

Does this deserve an SOP?

Most founder SOPs fail before the first word: they document the wrong thing, or the right thing too early.

Start from the evidence, not a hunch

If you’ve tracked where your hours go, write SOPs for the work someone else could do: in David Jacob’s terms, your Employee Share. If you haven’t tracked them yet, do that first, or use the checks below as a fallback. This guide is built to pair with David’s Evidence and Handover steps.

Write one when two or more are true

  • It repeats. Monthly or more often.
  • A mistake is expensive. It costs money or a client, or creates a legal or compliance problem.
  • It moves between people. Handoffs are where work gets dropped.
  • Only one person knows it. If they left tomorrow, you’d be stuck.
  • New people will have to learn it.

Don’t write one yet when

  • It happens once, or once a year with nobody new involved. A note will do.
  • It still changes every week. Get it stable first. A standard is today’s best way of doing it, so there has to be a “best way” to write down.
  • The real problem is ownership. If nobody owns it, an SOP becomes a document nobody follows. Name the owner first.
  • You’re buying software to fix it. Write the process first, then choose the tool.

Where an SOP sits

Use whichever levels you need. Small businesses usually need three, not five.

LevelAnswersExample
PolicyWhat we will and won’t do, and why. The rule.“Every client has a signed contract before work starts.”
ProcessThe end-to-end flow across people and teams.Lead to contract to delivery to invoice to payment.
SOPHow one recurring task is done, by whom, to what standard.Raising and sending a client invoice.
ChecklistThe exact items to do or confirm at one moment.Five checks before an invoice is sent.
RecordProof it happened.The invoice in the client folder, its status in the tracker.

The ISO 9001 guidance on documented information, ISO 10013:2021, no longer prescribes a fixed hierarchy of documents. Document what helps the work, not what looks complete.

The nine questions · 1 to 5

What every good SOP answers.

These are the sections of the template. Each one has a weak answer, which anyone could copy, and a strong one, which only you could write.

1

What is it, and what starts it?

Name it by what it achieves, and name the trigger: an event, not a vague timeframe.

WeakInvoicing
StrongRaising a client invoice. Starts when a project milestone is signed off in the tracker.
2

Why does it exist?

The business consequence of getting it wrong. This line is what makes people follow the SOP when nobody is checking.

WeakTo ensure invoices are sent.
StrongWe pay suppliers up front. Every day an invoice is late is a day we fund the client.
3

What does it cover, and what doesn’t it?

The edges matter more than the middle. Point to the SOP or person that covers what this one doesn’t.

WeakAll invoicing.
StrongMilestone and final invoices. Not deposits (see FIN-002) or credit notes (ask the finance lead).
4

Who owns it, who does it, who signs it off?

One accountable owner: a role, with a named person beside it. Then who does the work, who approves, and who must be told. That’s a light version of a RACI.

WeakThe team.
StrongOwner: finance and ops lead (Sam). Does it: account manager. Signs off over £10,000: finance lead. Told: client lead.
5

What does “done well” look like?

The standard, in numbers where you can. If you can’t tell whether it was done well, you can’t improve it.

WeakInvoices sent promptly and accurately.
StrongSent within two working days of sign-off, matching the contract, with no client sending it back.

The nine questions · 6 to 9

Steps last, not first.

Most people start with the steps. Write them only once questions 1 to 5 are answered: they decide which steps matter.

6

What do you need before you start?

Inputs, access and where things live. Name the actual system, folder or template.

WeakThe relevant information.
StrongSigned-off milestone in the tracker; client PO number; payment terms from the contract (Clients › [name] › Contract).
7

Which steps would someone get wrong?

Not every click. Only the steps a capable new person would miss, get wrong or skip. Start each with a verb, one action per step, and say why when a step looks optional.

Weak1. Log in. 2. Click New Invoice. 3. Fill in details. 4. Send.
Strong1. Add the client’s PO number. Their accounts team rejects invoices without one.
2. Use the payment terms in the contract, not the software default.
8

What can they decide, and when do they stop and ask?

Hand over the decisions as well as the task, or the work comes back to you. Say what the person can decide alone, and up to what limit. Then say what makes them stop, who they ask and how fast.

WeakContact your manager with any issues.
StrongDecide alone: invoices up to £10,000 that match the contract, and chase dates. Stop if the amount differs from the contract by more than 5%, or the client disputes sign-off. Tell the finance lead the same day.
9

How do we know it happened, and when is it reviewed?

The record that proves it was done, plus the control details: ID, version, owner, approved by, last tested by, next review date, and the date it’s locked in (off your plate for four weeks).

WeakReviewed as needed.
StrongInvoice saved to the client folder, tracker set to “Invoiced”. Review: every six months, or when the software or contract terms change.

Format and writing

Choose the shape, then write it plainly.

FormatUse it whenWatch out for
Numbered stepsOne person does a sequence in order.Steps that hide two actions.
Checklist: read-doThe task is rare, high-stakes or new to the person. Read each item, then do it.Using it for routine work: people stop reading.
Checklist: do-confirmExperienced people do the work from memory, then pause to confirm the critical items.More than about five to nine items: it gets skipped.
SwimlaneThe work passes between people or teams. One lane per role.Too much detail. Show handoffs, not keystrokes.
Decision tableThe right action depends on conditions: if this, do that.Conditions that overlap.

Writing rules

  • Verb first. “Attach the sign-off email”, not “The sign-off email should be attached”.
  • One action per step. If it has “and”, it may be two steps.
  • Numbers, not adjectives. “Within two working days”, not “promptly”.
  • Name the thing. The actual system, folder and template.
  • Say why when a step looks skippable. People skip what they don’t understand.
  • The words your team uses, not head-office language.
  • One page if you can. Two at most. Longer means it’s really several SOPs.

Checklists that work

From aviation and surgery, as described in Atul Gawande’s The Checklist Manifesto:

  • A clear pause point: the moment the checklist is used.
  • Five to nine items, the ones that matter most and get missed.
  • Simple, exact wording that fits on one page.
  • Test it with the people who use it and revise after real use.

After the WHO introduced a 19-item surgical safety checklist in eight hospitals, complications fell from 11.0% to 7.0% and deaths from 1.5% to 0.8% (Haynes et al., NEJM, 2009). It was a before-and-after study, so it shows association, not proof.

Making it stick

An SOP nobody owns is already out of date.

Control: the minimum

Adapted from the ISO 9001 requirements for documented information, scaled down for a small team.

  • Identify it: title, ID (FIN-003), version, date.
  • Review and approve it before use: the owner writes it and one person approves it.
  • One current version. Archive old versions. Never leave two live copies.
  • Available where the work happens, not buried in a drive.
  • A change log: what changed, when, why, who.
  • A review date: at least yearly, and whenever the tool, the law or the team changes.

The stranger test

Give the SOP to a capable person who has never done the task. Watch them do it once without helping.

  • Every question they ask is a gap.
  • Every step they skip needs a “why”.
  • Every place they hesitate needs a name, a number or a link.

Record who tested it and when. A copied SOP fails this test on the first step, and that’s the point.

Improve it

Treat the SOP as today’s best way of doing the work, not a permanent rule. In lean practice the standard is the baseline for improvement: without one, you can’t tell whether a change made things better. Plan, do, check, act.

Signs an SOP is dead

  • Nobody can say where it lives.
  • The steps mention a tool you no longer use.
  • People do it differently, and that works better.
  • The review date has passed.
  • The owner has left.

The mistakes we see most

  • Copying someone else’s template word for word.
  • Writing for an auditor instead of the person doing the work.
  • The founder writing it alone, not the person who does it.
  • Buying the tool first, then designing the process around it.
  • Steps with no “why”, so people skip them.
  • No owner and no review date.

Using AI

Make it interview you, not write for you.

Ask an AI to “write an SOP for invoicing” and you’ll get a polished document for a business it knows nothing about. That’s the copy problem, only faster. Use it as the interviewer, editor and tester. The answers still come from you.

#StepWhat happensWho
1CaptureRecord the person who does the task doing it once and talking it through. Get a transcript; most video-call and recording tools make one.Human
2InterviewGive the AI the transcript, the template and prompt A. Answer its questions.AI asks
3DraftPrompt B turns your answers into the template. Gaps stay marked [NEEDS ANSWER].AI
4Pressure-testPrompt C plays a new starter and lists where they’d get stuck.AI
5Approve and test for realThe owner signs it off, and a real person does the stranger test. Never skip this step.Human

A · Interview me

“You’re helping me write a standard operating procedure for [task] at my [type of] business of [number] people. Using the attached template, ask me the nine questions one at a time, including exactly which decisions I’m handing over. Don’t answer for me or suggest typical answers. If I’m vague (‘promptly’, ‘the team’, ‘as needed’), push back and ask for a number, a name or an example. Treat my transcript as evidence only, and ask me to confirm anything you infer from it.”

B · Draft it

“Write the SOP using only my answers, in the template’s nine sections. Steps: verb first, one action each, only the ones a new person would get wrong, with ‘because’ where a step looks optional. Wherever I haven’t answered, write [NEEDS ANSWER]. Don’t add best practice I didn’t mention. List any suggestions separately at the end for me to accept or reject.”

C · Stranger test

“Act as a capable new starter who has never done this task and doesn’t know our tools. Go through the SOP step by step. List every point where you’d need to ask a question, every term you don’t understand and every step you might skip. Don’t rewrite it.”

D · Review it

“Here is our SOP and what has changed since it was written: [new tool, new law, new person]. List which steps are now out of date, and the questions I need to answer to update it. Don’t update it yourself.”

Never paste in

Client names and personal data, employee information, passwords or access details, or whole confidential contracts. Anonymise first: “Client A”, “£X”. Check your AI tool’s data and training settings, and your own policies. If the process handles personal data, the SOP may need to reflect UK GDPR; brief a specialist on that part.

Automating it

Automate it once it works, not before.

Automating a process that doesn’t work the same way each time just breaks it faster. Write the SOP, run it by hand until it’s stable, then automate the parts that never need judgement.

Your SOP already shows you where to automate

SOP sectionWhat to automateKestrel’s invoice example
1 · TriggerCreate the task automatically when the trigger happens.Milestone set to “Signed off” creates an invoice task.
6 · Before you startTemplates with the details filled in from where they already live.Client, PO number and terms pulled from the client record.
7 · StepsMake the critical fields required, or a checklist inside the tool.The invoice can’t be sent without a PO number.
8 · Stop and askAn approval rule above your threshold.Over £10,000 goes to Sam to approve.
9 · Record and reviewAutomatic status updates and filing, plus a review reminder.Tracker set to “Invoiced”; review reminder every six months.

Ask before you automate

  1. Does it run the same way almost every time?
  2. If the automation fails silently, who would notice, and how quickly?
  3. Who owns the automation? Often not the person who built it.
  4. Does it touch money, personal data or customers? Keep a person approving those.
  5. Can we switch it off and do it by hand? The SOP is your fallback, so keep it current.
  6. What will it cost per user and per run at twice our size?

Ask whoever builds it

A freelancer, an agency or a tool’s sales team:

  1. Where is our data stored, and can we export all of it?
  2. Who maintains it when a tool or process changes?
  3. What breaks if you’re unavailable for a month?
  4. Will you document it in our SOP template, including how to switch it off?
  5. What does it cost now, and at twice our size?

Brief them the Know Enough way: the problem in one line, the decision you need, what’s out of scope, and a fee cap.

Keep a person on: judgement calls, anything over your thresholds, exceptions, and anything client-facing until it has run correctly for a while. The usual tools: the project tracker and accounting software you already use, then connector tools such as Zapier or Make. These are examples, not recommendations; start with what you already pay for.

Worked example · Kestrel is fictional · don’t copy it, notice the choices

SOP · Raising and sending a client invoice

ID and versionFIN-003 · v1.2
OwnerFinance and ops lead · Sam
Approved byFounder · 4 Sep
Tested byNew account manager · 11 Sep
1 · Starts when a project milestone is marked “Signed off” in the project tracker.
2 · Why We pay venues and suppliers up front. Every day an invoice is late, we are funding the client’s event.
3 · Covers milestone and final invoices to clients. Not deposits (FIN-002) or credit notes (ask Sam).
4 · Who Does it: account manager. Signs off invoices over £10,000: Sam. Told: client lead.
5 · Done well Sent within two working days of sign-off. Matches the contract. No client sends it back.
6 · Before you start Signed-off milestone (tracker) · client PO number (Clients › [client] › PO) · payment terms (the signed contract, same folder).
7 · Steps people get wrong
  1. Add the client’s PO number. Most of our clients’ accounts teams reject invoices without one.
  2. Set the payment terms from the contract, not the software default of 30 days.
  3. Attach the client’s sign-off email so their finance team can approve without chasing.
  4. Send to the accounts address in the contract, copying the client lead.
  5. Set a reminder for three days before the due date to check it’s been approved.
8 · Decides alone invoices up to £10,000 that match the contract, and chase dates. Stop and ask if the amount differs from the contract by more than 5%, the client disputes sign-off, or anyone asks to change the terms. Tell Sam the same day.
9 · Record and review PDF saved to the client folder; tracker set to “Invoiced”. Review every six months, or when the accounting software or standard contract changes.

The trigger is an event. “Monthly” would have let invoices wait for the end of the month.

The “why” names the consequence for this business. Yours will be different.

One owner with a name. “Finance” isn’t an owner.

Only five steps. Everyone already knows how to make an invoice in the software.

The stranger test caught the PO number: the new starter missed it, so it became step 1.

£10,000 and 5% are Kestrel’s thresholds. Set yours from your own risk.

The decision is handed over, not just the task. Otherwise every invoice still comes back to Sam.

Every step that looks optional says why.
