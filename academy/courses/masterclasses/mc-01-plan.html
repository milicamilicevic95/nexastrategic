1. The spine

The question. Bookkeeper, lawyer or ops hire: what does your business need now, and next?

The thesis. You don’t need to be the operator. You need to know enough to know when. Mila is the Swiss Army knife: a bit of every panel, and exactly where her limits are. Founders don’t need to become that. They need enough operating literacy to see the whole ball, know what their business needs now and next, and know when to bring someone in and what to ask.

The two tests. Now: the house isn’t on fire. I understand what’s happening, and the company can exist. Next: if I stepped away, what breaks, and where would we still need a person’s judgement?

The pivot. A business moves from Now to Next when it has paying clients in a clear customer profile and comfortable runway. From then on it needs a written strategic plan, and every panel answers to it. There’s no single formula; the closest established model is Churchill and Lewis’s five stages of small business growth (Harvard Business Review, 1983). At their “success” stage, an economically healthy business chooses between staying stable and committing to growth.

The thread. Kestrel, a fictional 35-person company built from Mila’s real engagements, runs through the whole hour.

What founders leave with. A Know Enough Map scored Now and Next, one red picked, and a four-part brief to send that week.

Guardrails

The not-advice line is said once, at the flip (slide 5), and printed on the map. Mila shows how to ask, never when someone needs legal, tax or financial advice. Triggers are tied to the business (runway, clients, headcount, contracts), not to the founder’s mindset, which is David’s ground. No firms are named. The audit trigger (slide 18) is presented with Mila’s interest declared. Kestrel is labelled fictional. Stories from real engagements are anonymised, with sectors changed and numbers rounded. Nothing said by Founders House members is reused elsewhere without consent.

2. Why this, and how it was tested

The market. Most operations masterclasses for founders sell “get the founder out of the way”, or values-to-process frameworks. MasterClass, the company, has business classes from well-known leaders, but I found no operations class. Its format is about 20 lessons of 10 to 12 minutes with a workbook. Its strength is the instructor’s own career stories; the criticisms are passive learning and “inspiration rather than a skill set”.

How Know Enough is different. It’s active (founders score the map and write a brief), precise (the question to ask, the smallest step first), practitioner-led, and monthly, so founders come back and re-score. A paid version sells an outcome (a scored map, a briefed red, feedback from Mila), not content, because MasterClass anchors what people expect to pay for content low.

The council. The spine went through two reviews by four advisory lenses: operator, sceptic, strategist and a founder in the audience. They agreed on the following:

  • the thesis
  • Now and Next taught live, with Later left to the map
  • one composite company as the thread
  • modular timing with the brief protected
  • “oversold”, never “swindled”
  • no pitch

The gap is in what was found, not proven. A one-question poll of Founders House members would confirm which framing they’ll show up for.

7nownext

The test · NOW · The house isn’t on fire. · I understand what’s happening, and the company can exist. · NEXT · If I stepped away, what breaks? · And where would we still need a person’s judgement?

Message
Now: the house isn’t on fire. Next: if I stepped away, what breaks?
Goal
Give them the two tests that structure the map.

Two tests, one for each horizon. Now: the house isn’t on fire. You understand what’s happening and the company can exist. Next: if you stepped away for a month, what would go wrong? Even with the best systems and automation, where would you still need a person, because it needs judgement nobody in the business has? Every row on your map has a Now column and a Next column.

8pivot

The pivot · From surviving to a written plan. · Paying clients in a clear market. Comfortable runway. Now the question is growth.

Message
You move to Next when you have paying clients in a clear market and runway; then you need a written plan.
Goal
Your point of view, with the closest research behind it.

There’s no single formula, and I looked. The closest is a classic model of small business growth (Churchill and Lewis, Harvard Business Review, 1983): at the “success” stage, a business that is economically healthy has a choice between staying stable and committing to growth. My version: you move from Now to Next when you have paying clients in a clear customer profile and comfortable runway. From that point, the business needs a written strategic plan, and every panel needs to answer to it. Kestrel reached this point and didn’t write the plan.

9tooearly

And the other way round · Operations can’t fix a product with no market. · Now comes first.

Message
Operations can’t fix a product with no market.
Goal
Stop anyone jumping to Next too early.

[Your story, anonymised: the early-stage platform in a high-risk regulated space where you de-risked everything, but it still had no market presence.] I called it putting a plaster on a leak. Operations can make a business safe and ready, but it can’t create demand. If you haven’t found your market, Next can wait; get Now right first.

11people story

Kestrel · People · Kestrel hired its way to ten times the size, with no hiring roadmap. · Teams duplicated each other. Niche roles multiplied. When funding tightened, there were redundancies.

Message
Hiring without a roadmap ends in duplication and redundancies.
Goal
Make the cost of skipping Next real.

[Your story: the scale-up you joined at about 35 people. No workforce plan, duplicated and very niche roles, and redundancies once investor funding was cut. Keep it anonymised and the numbers rounded.] Nobody did anything wrong on any single hire. There just wasn’t a plan the hires answered to.

12people

Panel 1 of 3 · People · Now · Contracts and right-to-work checks. · Payroll and pensions running. · Everyone knows their role. · Next · A hiring roadmap tied to the plan. · Every new role answers: why this, why now? · Who could help: · Payroll provider → HR adviser → People lead

Message
Now: contracts, payroll, roles. Next: a hiring roadmap tied to the plan.
Goal
They score their people row.

Now: contracts and right-to-work checks for everyone, payroll and workplace pensions running, everyone knows their role. UK basics; a professional confirms the specifics. Next: a hiring roadmap tied to the strategic plan. Step-away test: if you were gone for a month, would anyone know who to hire next, and why? Who could help, smallest step first: a payroll provider, an HR adviser, then a people lead. Score your row: Now and Next, red, amber or green.

13finance story

Kestrel · Finance · Kestrel’s hiring outran its money. · Every hire made sense on its own. Nobody had modelled what happens if the next round is late.

Message
Every hire made sense; nobody modelled the late round.
Goal
Connect people to money.

The money side of the same story. Payroll grew faster than the plan, because there wasn’t a plan, and there was no ‘what if’ case. [Your detail, if any.]

14finance

Panel 2 of 3 · Finance · Now · Books up to date every month. · You know this month’s cash. · Next · A 12-month cash plan. · A ‘what if the round doesn’t come’ case. · Who could help: · Bookkeeper → Accountant → Finance director, part-time → CFO

Message
Now: books and this month’s cash. Next: a 12-month plan with a contingency case.
Goal
They score their finance row.

Now: books up to date every month, and you know this month’s cash. Next: a 12-month cash plan with a contingency case: what happens if the next round is late or smaller? Who could help, smallest step first: a bookkeeper, an accountant, a part-time finance director, a CFO. Most businesses at this stage need the first two done well. Not financial advice: the point is knowing which conversation to have. Score your row: Now and Next, red, amber or green.

15legal story

Kestrel · Legal · A possible data incident. Specialists offered a full audit. · One precise question to a large firm got a step-by-step answer instead.

Message
One precise question beat a full audit.
Goal
The ‘don’t get oversold’ story, told carefully.

[Your story, anonymised and told carefully.] We had a possible data incident. Several specialists wanted to come in and run a full audit. We asked one large firm one question: what are the steps, in order, and what happens in the worst case? We got a step-by-step answer. The lesson is the precise question. One fact founders must know: with personal data, the clock matters. Under UK GDPR, if a breach is likely to put people at risk, it must be reported to the ICO within 72 hours of becoming aware of it. So the first question to a specialist is whether it’s reportable, today. Don’t tell this story in a way that sounds like avoiding that clock.

16legal

Panel 3 of 3 · Legal · Now · Core contracts signed and read. · Data protection basics in place. · Next · A plan for when something goes wrong. · You know who you call first. · Who could help: · Templates → Solicitor, one question → Specialist

Message
Now: contracts read, data basics. Next: a plan for when it goes wrong.
Goal
They score their legal row. You show how to ask, not when to take advice.

Now: your core contracts are signed and you’ve read them, and data protection basics are in place. Next: a plan for when something goes wrong, including who you call first. Who could help, smallest step first: good templates, a solicitor for one question, a specialist when it’s their area. I won’t tell you when you need legal advice; that’s for a solicitor. I’ll show you how to ask. Score your row: Now and Next, red, amber or green.

17islands

Kestrel · The whole ball · Every team its own island. · Rev ops, fin ops, legal ops. Operations was called in when things broke.

Message
When operations is split into islands, nobody sees the whole ball.
Goal
Bring the ball back.

[Your story: a small operations team, while rev ops, fin ops, legal ops, marketing ops and event ops each worked alone. You were deployed when something went wrong: “Can you go and figure out what’s going on?”] That’s the ball cut into colours. Nobody saw the whole thing until it broke.

18audit

The last trigger · When you can afford it, get the whole ball looked at. · A neutral operator, an audit, and a view of what’s missing. Take it or leave it.

Message
When you can afford it, have the whole ball looked at.
Goal
The last trigger, with your interest declared.

Say plainly that this is the kind of work you do, so nobody thinks it’s hidden. My view: once you have the free cash to bring in a contractor, have a generalist operator audit the business first. You get a report: where you are, what’s missing, what’s siloed, what I’d recommend. Feedback is an opinion, and you don’t have to take it. Founders often don’t enjoy being audited. It’s still the cheapest way to see the whole ball before it costs you.

The brief (11 minutes)

20p3

The brief · Ask one precise question.

Message
Knowing who to call is half of it.
Goal
Move from knowing to asking.

About 11 minutes. Knowing who to call is half of it. The other half is what you say when you call. That’s how Kestrel got a step-by-step answer instead of an audit.

21brief

Not this · “Can you have a look at our data situation?” · This · The problem, in one line. · The decision I need. · What’s out of scope. · A fee cap, and by when.

Message
A good brief has four parts.
Goal
A tool they’ll use next week.

A vague brief gets a big answer and a big bill. Four parts: the problem in one line, the decision you need to make with their answer, what you are not asking for, a fee cap and a deadline. This is the back of your map.

22rewrite

Your turn · Kestrel’s brief · “We need some help with HR.” · Rewrite it. Problem, decision, scope, fee cap.

Message
You can do this in two minutes.

Practise on Kestrel, not on anyone’s own business.

Live, about 6 minutes. Use Kestrel, not anyone’s own situation: nobody has to share their business in front of the room. Ask for rewrites, take two or three, improve one together. A strong answer: “Funding is tighter and we have overlapping roles in two teams. We need to know the fair process for a restructure before we decide whether to start one. 4. Before the day

  1. Anonymise the stories. The scale-up (slides 11, 13, 17), the possible data incident (15) and the early-stage platform (9). Change the sectors, round the numbers, and leave out anything David or a member could recognise.
  2. Tell the data incident story carefully. The lesson is the precise question. Don’t frame it as avoiding calling something a breach. Under UK GDPR, a personal data breach likely to put people at risk must be reported to the ICO within 72 hours of becoming aware of it. The ICO’s guidance is under review following the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, so check the live page before the day.
  3. Ryanair (slide 19). Keep to what’s publicly reported or what you’ve observed. Avoid specific claims about their internal teams unless you can source them.
  4. Have an accountant and a solicitor sense-check the map.
  5. Send the map 48 hours ahead. Add how founders join the course (slide 25).
  6. Check the boundary with David. The pivot is kept operational, but confirm he’s comfortable with it. Confirm IP in the materials stays with you (decision d07).
