Taboo trade-offs and sacred values

“Some things aren't for sale, and offering money for them makes it worse.”

Taboo trade-offs and sacred values. Some values people refuse to weigh against money, and offering money for them causes outrage instead of agreement.

In full

People treat some values (loyalty, identity, fairness, the founding mission) as sacred and refuse to weigh them against money or convenience. Proposing such a trade produces moral outrage, and the person who proposed it can lose standing. In conflicts over sacred values, adding a material sweetener can harden opposition, while symbolic gestures such as an apology or an acknowledgement can soften it.

Where it comes from

Philip E. Tetlock, Orie V. Kristel, S. Beth Elson, Melanie C. Green and Jennifer S. Lerner, 'The psychology of the unthinkable: Taboo trade-offs, forbidden base rates, and heretical counterfactuals', Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 78(5), 2000, pp. 853-870. Philip E. Tetlock, 'Thinking the unthinkable: Sacred values and taboo cognitions', Trends in Cognitive Sciences 7(7), 2003, pp. 320-324 (doi 10.1016/S1364-6613(03)00135-9). Jeremy Ginges, Scott Atran, Douglas Medin and Khalil Shikaki, 'Sacred bounds on rational resolution of violent political conflict', PNAS 104(18), 2007.

The moment

An acquirer offers a co-founder a larger earn-out to drop the commitment that the product stays free for charities. The more money goes on the table, the more insulted the founder feels.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Bigger offers producing more anger
  • 'Principle', 'never' or 'insulting' in the reply
  • A sticking point tied to mission, identity or loyalty

Ask in the room

Is there something in this deal they would see as betraying what they stand for?

In a founder-led company

An acquirer offers a co-founder a larger earn-out to drop the commitment that the product will stay free for charities. The more money goes on the table, the more insulted the founder feels. The deal moves forward only once the acquirer puts the commitment in writing and acknowledges publicly why it mattered.

What it costs

Negotiations harden and the person making the offer loses standing, often without seeing why. Deals stall that an acknowledgement or a written commitment could have moved.

Try this week

Before your next hard negotiation, write down which of the other side's positions might be sacred to them. For each, plan an acknowledgement or written commitment instead of a sweetener.

What to change

Use it in acquisitions, restructures and any change touching the founding mission. Separate sacred issues from tradeable ones, meet the first with acknowledgement and commitments, and keep money for the second.

Rating

Robust Tetlock et al., Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2000, and Ginges et al., PNAS, 2007. Found across several studies and populations, though mostly from hypothetical vignettes and surveys rather than observed negotiations.

Right or wrong?

Proven, with a caveat. Outrage at taboo trade-offs, and the backfire of material offers on sacred issues, show up across studies and populations, though mostly in vignettes and surveys. 'Sacred values never move' overstates it: people often accept the trade once it is framed as routine or as sacred against sacred.

Why this rating

Moral outrage at taboo trade-offs, and the backfire effect of material offers on sacred issues, have been found across several studies and populations (including Israeli, Palestinian and later Iranian samples), although most of the evidence comes from hypothetical vignettes and surveys rather than observed negotiations.

Choice overload (the paradox of choice)

“Too many options, so nobody decides”

Choice overload. With many options, people sometimes choose less or regret more. It happens mainly when options are complex, preferences are unclear and someone has to commit to one.

In full

Faced with many options, people sometimes choose less, choose worse or regret more than with a few. The famous demonstration: shoppers who saw 24 jams were far less likely to buy than those who saw 6. Later research shows the effect is real only under specific conditions: complex options, unclear preferences, time pressure and the need to commit to one final choice.

Where it comes from

Iyengar, S.S. and Lepper, M.R. (2000) 'When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing?', Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(6), 995-1006: 30 per cent bought from the 6-jam display versus 3 per cent from the 24-jam display. Popularised by Barry Schwartz, The Paradox of Choice (2004). Meta-analyses: Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R. and Todd, P.M. (2010) 'Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload', Journal of Consumer Research, 37(3), 409-425; Chernev, A., Böckenholt, U. and Goodman, J. (2015) 'Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis', Journal of Consumer Psychology, 25(2), 333-358.

The moment

The pricing page had five tiers, three add-ons and two billing options. Trial users kept stalling there.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Long option lists with no recommendation
  • 'I need more time to think'
  • Decisions made by default because nobody chose

Ask in the room

Which option do we recommend, and what are the criteria?

In a founder-led company

A founder launches with five pricing tiers, three add-ons and a choice of monthly or annual billing. Trial users stall on the plan page. Cutting to three tiers with one marked as recommended lifts conversion, because buyers new to the category do not yet know what they want. The same thing happens internally: twelve strategic options at an offsite produce debate, not a decision.

What it costs

Customers and teams delay. The cost shows up as stalled conversions, endless offsite debates and decisions made by default.

Try this week

Take one decision you're presenting this week. Cut it to three options and mark the one you recommend.

What to change

Use it on pricing pages, hiring shortlists, offsites and proposals. Agree the criteria before showing the options, and always recommend one.

Rating

Contested Iyengar and Lepper's jam study, 2000. A 50-study meta-analysis found no average effect; a later one found it under four specific conditions. Decision fatigue evidence is weak.

Right or wrong?

Your instinct is half right. More options don't paralyse people by default. Complex options, offered to someone unsure what they want and under time pressure, do. 'Decision fatigue' is weaker still: the willpower theory behind it failed a 23-lab replication.

Why this rating

Scheibehenne et al. pooled 50 experiments with 5,036 people and found an average effect of virtually zero, while Chernev et al. found it appears reliably under four conditions: complex choice sets, difficult tasks, uncertain preferences and a goal of picking one option.

The hiding hand

“If we'd known how hard it would be, we'd never have started”

The hiding hand. Albert Hirschman argued we underestimate both how hard projects are and how inventive we'll be, and the two errors can cancel out. Often they don't.

In full

Albert Hirschman's argument that people underestimate both how hard a project will be and how inventive they will be when problems appear, and that the two errors can cancel out: ignorance gets projects started that people then find ways to finish. It is the optimistic mirror image of the planning fallacy. Flyvbjerg and Sunstein named the opposite case the malevolent hiding hand, where costs are underestimated and no saving creativity arrives.

Where it comes from

Albert O. Hirschman, 'The principle of the hiding hand', The Public Interest, Winter 1967, and Development Projects Observed (Brookings Institution, 1967), based on 11 World Bank-financed projects. Test: Flyvbjerg, B. and Sunstein, C.R. (2016) 'The principle of the malevolent hiding hand; or, the planning fallacy writ large', Social Research, 83(4). Reply: Ika, L.A. (2018) 'Beneficial or detrimental ignorance: The straw man fallacy of Flyvbjerg's test of Hirschman's Hiding Hand', World Development, 103, 369-382.

The moment

The COO warned that building in-house would take three times the estimate. The founder went ahead, partly because she didn't know enough to be scared.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • 'We'll figure it out' as the plan
  • Optimism about cost and capability at once
  • Stories of founders who got lucky

Ask in the room

What exactly are we counting on to solve the problems we haven't seen yet?

In a founder-led company

A founder is warned that building the platform in-house will take three times the estimate and goes ahead anyway, partly because she does not know enough to be frightened. Whether that turns out to be the hiding hand or its malevolent twin depends on a question the board can ask now: when the obstacles appear, which specific capability or resource are we counting on to solve them, and do we have it?

What it costs

When the saving creativity doesn't arrive, you are committed to a project costing far more than planned.

Try this week

For your boldest current bet, name the specific skill, person or resource you're relying on to solve problems you can't yet see.

What to change

Use it when a team wants to start something ambitious. Allow the boldness, then test whether the problem-solving capacity is real.

Rating

Contested Hirschman, 1967, from 11 projects. Flyvbjerg and Sunstein found the bad version in 78 per cent of 327 projects; Ika (2018) disputes their test.

Right or wrong?

Contested. Flyvbjerg and Sunstein found the benevolent version in only 22 per cent of 327 large projects, while Ika, using broader measures across 161 World Bank projects, found it prevailing. Hirschman never claimed ignorance is good in general; the answer depends on how you define success.

Why this rating

Flyvbjerg and Sunstein found the benevolent version in 22 per cent and the malevolent one in 78 per cent of 327 large projects with both cost and benefit data, while Ika, using broader measures across 161 World Bank projects, found the hiding hand prevailing, so the answer depends on how success is defined.

Loss aversion

“Losing hurts more than winning feels good”

Loss aversion. A loss usually weighs more with us than a gain of the same size.

In full

People judge outcomes as gains or losses against a reference point, not as final states of wealth, and on average a loss weighs more than a gain of the same size. Loss aversion is the size of that gap, usually expressed as a coefficient (lambda). How large it is, and whether it holds for small or routine stakes, depends on context.

Where it comes from

Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979) 'Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk', Econometrica 47(2), 263-291. They described a value function that is 'steeper for losses than for gains' but gave no coefficient. A riskless version followed in Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D. (1991) 'Loss Aversion in Riskless Choice: A Reference-Dependent Model', Quarterly Journal of Economics 106(4), 1039-1061. The often-quoted lambda of 2.25 is the median estimate in Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D. (1992) 'Advances in Prospect Theory: Cumulative Representation of Uncertainty', Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 5, 297-323.

The moment

The founder won't close a product line losing £40k a month because it would 'write off' two years of work. Yet they would never spend £40k a month to start the same line today.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Talk of 'writing off' past work
  • Losing projects kept alive with no fresh case
  • Decisions measured from where you started, not from zero

Ask in the room

If we weren't already doing this, would we start it today at this cost?

In a founder-led company

A founder will not close a product line that is losing £40k a month because closing it 'writes off' two years of work, yet would never spend £40k a month to start the same line today. The board discussion turns on whether to measure the decision from the sunk position or from zero.

What it costs

Money keeps going into losing lines because stopping feels like a loss, while the same spend on a new idea would be turned down.

Try this week

Take one loss-making product or project and write the case for starting it today from zero. If you wouldn't start it, set a closure date.

What to change

Use it in quarterly portfolio reviews. Judge every ongoing line on its future cost and return, as if you were deciding whether to start it now.

Rating

Contested Kahneman and Tversky, Econometrica, 1979, introduced it, and a 2024 meta-analysis puts the average coefficient near 2, but estimates vary widely and some studies find no effect for small, symmetric stakes.

Right or wrong?

Partly. On average losses do weigh more than equal gains, and a 2024 meta-analysis puts the ratio near 2. But 'losses hurt twice as much' isn't a fixed law: the size varies widely, is smaller among people who know the domain, and some studies find no effect for small amounts.

Why this rating

A 2024 meta-analysis of 607 estimates puts the mean coefficient at 1.955 (95% CI 1.82-2.10), but estimates vary widely: Gal & Rucker (2018) argue that losses do not reliably outweigh gains, Mrkva et al. (2020, N=17,720) find the effect is real but smaller among people with domain knowledge, and later replications find none for small amounts presented symmetrically.

The endowment effect

“"It's worth more because it's mine"; why nobody will give up the thing they built”

The endowment effect. People tend to want more to give something up than they would pay to get it if they didn't already own it.

In full

People tend to demand more to give up something they own than they would pay to get the same thing if they did not own it. In lab terms, willingness to accept (WTA) exceeds willingness to pay (WTP), and fewer trades happen than standard theory predicts. How big the gap is, and why it exists, depends a great deal on the goods and the procedure used.

Where it comes from

Term coined by Richard H. Thaler, 'Toward a positive theory of consumer choice', Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization 1(1), 39-60, March 1980, doi:10.1016/0167-2681(80)90051-7 (built on hypothetical vignettes). Experimental demonstration: Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 'Experimental tests of the endowment effect and the Coase theorem', Journal of Political Economy 98(6), 1325-1348, December 1990, doi:10.1086/261737 (the mugs studies). Main challenge: Plott & Zeiler, 'The willingness to pay-willingness to accept gap, the "endowment effect", subject misconceptions, and experimental procedures for eliciting valuations', American Economic Review 95(3), 530-545, 2005, doi:10.1257/0002828054201387.

The moment

Before a pivot, the founder values the legacy product line far above what she would pay to buy it today. It survives two more planning cycles than the numbers justify.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Legacy products defended with history rather than numbers
  • 'We built it ourselves' as the case for keeping it
  • Kill decisions pushed to the next planning cycle

Ask in the room

If a competitor owned this, what would we pay to buy it today?

In a founder-led company

Before a pivot, a founder prices the legacy product line, or the in-house tool the first engineers built, far above what they would pay to acquire it today. So it survives two planning cycles longer than the numbers justify.

What it costs

Money and people stay tied to products and tools the numbers no longer support, and pivots happen later than they should.

Try this week

Pick one product, tool or project you own. Write down what you would pay to acquire it today, then what you'd accept to give it up, and look at the gap.

What to change

Use it at pivots, portfolio reviews and build-versus-buy decisions. Ask someone with no stake in the thing to price it, and decide on that number.

Rating

Contested Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler, Journal of Political Economy, 1990. The gap between selling and buying prices replicates under standard procedures, but Plott and Zeiler (2005) showed procedure can remove it, so its size and cause are disputed.

Right or wrong?

Partly proven. People usually do ask more to sell than they would pay to buy, but the gap shrinks or vanishes under some procedures, and the popular explanation, loss aversion, is not settled.

Why this rating

The WTA/WTP gap replicates under standard procedures, and Fehr, Hakimov & Kübler (2015) failed to reproduce Plott & Zeiler's no-gap result for mugs, but Plott & Zeiler showed that training and procedure can remove it, so its size and its link to loss aversion remain disputed.

Satisficing

“Good enough is good enough; stop shopping once something clears the bar”

Satisficing. Setting a bar for what's good enough and taking the first option that clears it, instead of searching for the best possible one.

In full

Satisficing means setting an aspiration level and taking the first option that meets it, instead of searching for the best possible option. Herbert Simon argued that real decision makers have limited time, information and computing power, so in practice they adapt well enough to 'satisfice' and do not optimise. It is a description of how decisions are actually made under constraints, not an instruction to lower standards.

Where it comes from

Herbert A. Simon, 'Rational choice and the structure of the environment', Psychological Review 63(2), 129-138 (1956), doi:10.1037/h0042769, which contains the line that organisms 'adapt well enough to "satisfice"; they do not, in general, "optimize"'. The underlying model is in Simon, 'A behavioral model of rational choice', Quarterly Journal of Economics 69(1) (1955); the phrase 'bounded rationality' is usually dated to Models of Man (Wiley, 1957). The popular maximiser/satisficer split comes from Schwartz, Ward, Monterosso, Lyubomirsky, White and Lehman, 'Maximizing versus satisficing: Happiness is a matter of choice', Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 83(5), 1178-1197 (2002).

The moment

The founder has interviewed eleven candidates for Head of Finance over four months, rejecting each against an unwritten ideal. Meanwhile month-end close slips and the board pack goes out late.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Searches running without written criteria
  • Candidates rejected for reasons nobody can state
  • Decisions reopened whenever a new option appears

Ask in the room

What are the three things this person must be able to do in their first 90 days?

In a founder-led company

A founder has interviewed eleven candidates for a Head of Finance role over four months, rejecting each against an unwritten ideal, while month-end close slips and the board pack goes out late. Writing down the three things the hire must be able to do in the first 90 days, and offering to the first candidate who clears that bar, turns an open-ended search into a decision.

What it costs

An open-ended search for the best option costs time the business doesn't have, and the work the hire should be doing goes undone. An unwritten bar also makes every rejection hard to explain.

Try this week

For one stalled decision, write the minimum bar in three lines. Commit to the first option that clears it.

What to change

Use it for decisions where delay costs more than a slightly better choice, which covers most hires, suppliers and tools. Agree the bar in writing before the search starts.

Rating

Contested Simon, Psychological Review, 1956. His account of bounded search is foundational and widely accepted, but the popular 'maximisers are unhappier' finding rests on a scale later studies found psychometrically weak.

Right or wrong?

Simon's core idea holds: real decisions are made under limits of time, information and computing power, and people stop at good enough. The popular add-on that maximisers are unhappier is contested, because the scale behind it has measurement problems.

Why this rating

Simon's core claim about bounded search is foundational and widely accepted, but the popular 'maximisers are unhappier' finding rests on the Schwartz 2002 Maximization Scale, which later studies (Nenkov et al. 2008; Diab, Gillespie and Highhouse 2008; Rim et al. 2011) found psychometrically weak and multidimensional, with the unhappiness link driven largely by the decision-difficulty and alternative-search items.

Escalation of commitment

“We've come too far to stop now”

Escalation of commitment. We keep investing in a failing course of action because we chose it, and stopping feels like admitting the mistake.

In full

Continuing, or increasing, investment in a failing course of action because you were responsible for choosing it, despite evidence that it is not working. It overlaps with the sunk cost effect but is broader: the drivers include self-justification, reputation, project momentum and organisational politics, not only an aversion to 'wasting' past spend. Staw's original finding was that personal responsibility for the earlier decision increased further investment after negative results.

Where it comes from

Staw, B.M. (1976) 'Knee-deep in the big muddy: A study of escalating commitment to a chosen course of action', Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 16(1), 27-44, doi:10.1016/0030-5073(76)90005-2 (role-play with 240 business students). Sunk cost effect: Arkes, H.R. and Blumer, C. (1985) 'The psychology of sunk cost', Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 35(1), 124-140 (including the Ohio University theatre season-ticket field study). Meta-analysis: Sleesman, D.J., Conlon, D.E., McNamara, G. and Miles, J.E. (2012) 'Cleaning up the big muddy: A meta-analytic review of the determinants of escalation of commitment', Academy of Management Journal, 55(3), 541-562, doi:10.5465/amj.2010.0696.

The moment

Eighteen months into the enterprise pivot, the founder approved one more senior hire and one more pilot. Win rates were still nowhere near plan.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Every review calls bad results 'early'
  • The sponsor is the only judge
  • Talk of what's been spent, not what's ahead

Ask in the room

If we were starting fresh today, would we fund this?

In a founder-led company

Eighteen months into a pivot to enterprise, the founder who championed it keeps approving one more senior hire and one more pilot, even though win rates and sales cycles are well outside the plan. The co-founder who opposed the pivot can see it, but the founder's credibility with the board is now tied to the strategy, so every quarterly review reframes the evidence as 'early'.

What it costs

Cash, people and board trust keep flowing to a decision that has already failed, judged by the person who chose it.

Try this week

For one big initiative, write down today the result that would make you stop, and the date you'll check it.

What to change

Set stop criteria at the start of every major bet. Have someone who didn't champion it review progress against them.

Rating

Contested Staw, 1976; meta-analysis by Sleesman et al., 2012. Escalation is real and has several causes, but replications of Staw's original effect are mixed.

Right or wrong?

Real, but not for one simple reason. People do keep investing in failing courses of action, driven by reputation, politics and momentum as well as sunk costs. Staw's specific claim, that personal responsibility drives it, has mixed replications.

Why this rating

The meta-analysis shows escalation is real and multi-causal, but direct replications of Staw's personal-responsibility effect are mixed (Białek et al. 2021 found no escalation with N = 365; a 2025 preregistered PSPB replication found only partial support), and the Arkes and Blumer field result rests on a small sample.

Status quo bias

“"If it isn't broken, don't fix it" / "We've always done it this way"”

Status quo bias. People stick with the current arrangement, or the default option, more often than its merits justify.

In full

People stick with the current option, or whatever is pre-selected, more often than its merits justify. The pull is strongest when changing takes effort, carries visible risk of regret, or the default looks like an implicit recommendation. Defaults are the most studied form: changing what happens when nobody acts can shift outcomes a great deal.

Where it comes from

Named and formalised by William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser, "Status quo bias in decision making", Journal of Risk and Uncertainty, 1(1), 7-59 (March 1988), DOI 10.1007/BF00055564. It combined decision experiments with field data on Harvard employees' health plan choices and TIAA-CREF retirement allocations. Default effect field evidence: Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea, "The power of suggestion: Inertia in 401(k) participation and savings behavior", Quarterly Journal of Economics, 116(4), 1149-1187 (2001), first circulated as NBER Working Paper 7682 (2000). Organ donation: Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein, "Do defaults save lives?", Science, 302, 1338-1339 (2003).

The moment

The founder still approves every hire above mid-level, though nobody formally decided to keep it that way. When the COO proposes the new VP Sales as default approver, the founder asks for a case for change, and nobody asks for a case for keeping things as they are.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Processes nobody remembers deciding to keep
  • Business cases required for change, never for continuing
  • 'We've always done it this way' as the main argument

Ask in the room

If we were setting this up from scratch today, would we choose the current arrangement?

In a founder-led company

The Dunning-Kruger effect

In Kruger and Dunning's studies the weakest performers on a task overestimated their performance most, while the strongest slightly underestimated theirs. Kruger, J. and Dunning, D. Bystander effect. When other people are present, each person is less likely to act, because responsibility feels shared.

In full

When other people are present, each individual is less likely to act, because responsibility feels shared and the cost of doing nothing is spread across the group. The effect is about the probability that a given individual acts, not that a group acts. It weakens markedly when the danger is clear and serious.

Where it comes from

Darley, J. M. & Latané, B. (1968) 'Bystander intervention in emergencies: Diffusion of responsibility', Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 8(4), 377-383. In the intercom 'seizure' experiment, participants who believed more people were listening were slower and less likely to help. The study was prompted by press coverage of the murder of Kitty Genovese in Queens in March 1964.

The individual-level effect replicates in lab studies (Fischer et al. 2011 meta-analysis, 105 effect sizes, more than 7,700 participants, g = -0.35), but it shrinks in dangerous emergencies, and CCTV of 219 real public conflicts found at least one bystander intervened in about nine in ten, more often when more people were present (Philpot et al. 2020).

A group takes an action that contradicts what each member privately wants, because everyone misreads everyone else's preferences and keeps quiet. “The meeting was about something else entirely”

In full

Wilfred Bion argued that every group runs on two levels at once: the work group, pursuing its stated task, and a basic assumption group, acting out an unspoken emotional agenda. Bion, W.R. (1961) Experiences in Groups and Other Papers, London: Tavistock Publications, collecting papers from 1948 to 1951. The Tavistock Institute ran the first Leicester group relations conference in 1957 and still runs it. Later additions: Pierre Turquet's 'oneness' (1974) and Gordon Lawrence, Alastair Bain and Laurence Gould's 'me-ness' (1996). Revisited in French, R.B. and Simpson, P. If promotion depends on performance in the current role rather than fit for the next one, people keep rising until they reach a job they are poor at, and then they stay there. A direct replication in Germany found compliance as high as the original, but Feeley et al. (2012) found the technique reliably increases verbal agreement while its effect on actual follow-through is small (r of about .13), and the reciprocal concessions mechanism has rivals.

The Trollope ploy

“Answer the offer you want, not the one they last sent.”

The Trollope ploy. When you receive two versions of an offer, you reply to the one you prefer as if the other never arrived.

In full

On 26 October 1962 Khrushchev sent Kennedy a long, private, conciliatory letter offering to remove the missiles from Cuba in exchange for a US pledge not to invade. On 27 October a harder second message, broadcast publicly on Moscow radio, added the demand that US Jupiter missiles be withdrawn from Turkey. The ExComm replied as if only the first letter existed. That evening Robert Kennedy also told Ambassador Dobrynin privately that the Jupiters would be removed within months, on condition that this was never made public. The pattern is selective acceptance: you set the terms by deciding which offer to treat as real. The full story shows the ploy succeeded alongside a concession, not instead of one.

Where it comes from

The name refers to a recurring device in Anthony Trollope's novels, in which a woman deliberately reads a casual gesture, such as a squeeze of the hand, as a proposal of marriage. The credit to Robert Kennedy began with Stewart Alsop and Charles Bartlett's December 1962 Saturday Evening Post article. RFK's posthumous memoir, Thirteen Days (1969), reinforced it. Dean Rusk's memoir and Robert McNamara (including in The Fog of War, 2003) credited Llewellyn Thompson. Sheldon Stern's analyses of the ExComm tapes (for example Averting 'The Final Failure', 2003, and The Week the World Stood Still, 2005) show the idea emerging from several participants, with Bundy, Thompson and Sorensen all voicing it. Sorensen drafted the reply.

  • A harder offer arriving after their partnership meeting
  • A deal closing faster than the stated terms explain

Ask in the room

In a founder-led company

A founder receives two term sheets from the same lead investor a day apart: the first is founder-friendly, the second, after their partnership meeting, adds a 2x liquidation preference. Told as the whole story, it hides the side concession that actually closed the deal, and the board decides without knowing what was given away.

Partly true. The US did answer Khrushchev's first letter and ignore the second, but the credit to Robert Kennedy alone is a myth, and the ploy didn't end the crisis by itself: a secret promise to remove the Jupiter missiles from Turkey went with it.

That the US answered the first letter is documented, but who originated the idea is disputed, and the tapes and the Dobrynin channel show the ploy was not on its own what ended the crisis.

Chesterton's fence

“Don't take a fence down until you know why it was put up”

Chesterton's fence. Before you remove a rule or process whose purpose you can't see, find out why it was put there.

In full

Before removing a rule, process or structure whose purpose you cannot see, find out why it was put there. Chesterton's point is about who carries the burden of proof: the reformer who says 'I don't see the use of this' has to establish the use before tearing it down. It is not an argument for keeping things. Chesterton allows that once you understand the reason, you may still remove it.

Where it comes from

G. K. Chesterton, The Thing (London: Sheed & Ward, 1929), chapter 'The Drift from Domesticity', p. 35 in the 1929 London edition. Original wording: 'There exists in such a case a certain institution or law; let us say, for the sake of simplicity, a fence or gate erected across a road. The more modern type of reformer goes gaily up to it and says, "I don't see the use of this; let us clear it away." To which the more intelligent type of reformer will do well to answer: "If you don't see the use of it, I certainly won't let you clear it away. Go away and think. Then, when you can come back and tell me that you do see the use of it, I may allow you to destroy it."' The short form, 'Don't ever take a fence down until you know why it was put up', is a later paraphrase. Bartlett's Familiar Quotations ascribes it to John F. Kennedy, who attributed it to Chesterton in a 1945 notebook.

Folk wisdom, and usually misquoted. Chesterton did not say never take a fence down: he said find out why it is there, and then you may remove it. Nobody has tested how often such fences turn out to matter.

This is a rhetorical heuristic from an essay with no empirical test behind it. Its value is as a prompt to investigate, and nobody has measured it against a base rate of fences that turned out to be useless.

In full

That outcome, called backfire, is the extreme end of the rebound effect, where some but not all of the expected saving is eaten up by extra use. Whether full backfire happens depends on how much demand responds to lower cost and how central the resource is to the wider economy.

Where it comes from

Daniel Khazzoom, "Economic implications of mandated efficiency standards for household appliances", Energy Journal (1980); Leonard Brookes, "The greenhouse effect: The fallacies in the energy efficiency solution", Energy Policy (1990). In full

The cobra effect

“The incentive made the problem worse”

The cobra effect. An incentive tied to a proxy makes the real problem worse, because people produce the proxy instead of the outcome.

In full

The story: a British official in colonial Delhi paid a bounty for dead cobras, people bred cobras for the money, the bounty was scrapped and the breeders released their stock, leaving more snakes than before. The better-documented case is Hanoi in 1902, where the French colonial administration paid per rat tail and people cut tails off live rats and released them to breed, or farmed rats outright. The pattern: when you pay for a proxy, people produce the proxy rather than the outcome.

Schismogenesis

“Each side's reaction makes the other side worse; we've become each other's opposite”

In full

Schismogenesis is a process in which two parties respond to each other's behaviour in a way that pushes both further apart over time. In the symmetrical form, equals escalate the same behaviour (boast against boast, rivalry against rivalry); in the complementary form, opposite behaviours feed each other (dominance draws submission, which draws more dominance). Left unchecked, either form can break the relationship unless something interrupts the loop.

Where it comes from

The symmetrical/complementary distinction was carried into communication theory and family therapy by the Palo Alto group, notably Watzlawick, Beavin and Jackson, Pragmatics of Human Communication (1967). Graeber and Wengrow revived it as 'cultural schismogenesis' between neighbouring societies in The Dawn of Everything (2021).

Leicester City's 2015-16 Premier League title

Leicester City's 2015-16 title. A 5,000-1 outsider won the Premier League through a mix of real advantages and favourable luck that did not repeat.

In full

Moneyball

“Buy what the market underprices”

Moneyball. Winning by buying a skill the market underprices, until rivals notice and the price goes up.

In full

In 2002 the Oakland Athletics, with one of baseball's lowest payrolls, won 103 games and set an American League record with a 20-game winning streak. Under general manager Billy Beane and assistant Paul DePodesta, they used statistical analysis to hire players whose skills, chiefly getting on base, the market undervalued. The pattern is arbitrage on a mispriced attribute. It is also a lesson in how fast the edge goes once rivals copy it.

Where it comes from

Michael Lewis, Moneyball: The Art of Winning an Unfair Game (W. W. Norton, 2003). The analytical roots are Bill James's self-published Baseball Abstract from 1977 and the 'sabermetrics' coined from SABR, the Society for American Baseball Research. Within the A's, the approach began under Beane's predecessor Sandy Alderson. The evidence test is Jahn K. Hakes and Raymond D. Sauer, 'An Economic Evaluation of the Moneyball Hypothesis', Journal of Economic Perspectives 20(3), 173-186 (2006), doi:10.1257/jep.20.3.173.

The moment

A seed-stage founder hires strong engineers returning from career breaks, whom other firms' CV filters screen out. Three years later two well-funded competitors start the same programme, and her offer acceptance rate drops.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Competitors start copying your hiring or pricing approach
  • A once-cheap advantage starts costing more
  • A model that worked for years suddenly underperforms

Ask in the room

Which of our advantages depends on others not seeing it, and how would we know when they do?

In a founder-led company

A seed-stage founder can't match big-tech salaries, so she hires strong engineers who are returning from career breaks and screened out by other firms' CV filters. It works for three years. Then two well-funded competitors start the same returner programme, her offer acceptance rate drops, and the board asks why the hiring model has 'stopped working'.

What it costs

The edge fades once rivals copy it, and the board can mistake a closed gap in the market for a broken model.

Try this week

List your main advantages and, for each, note whether a competitor could copy it within a year. Pick one early sign of copying to track.

What to change

Use it when an advantage rests on something the market misprices. Plan for the gap to close and keep looking for the next underpriced attribute.

Rating

Contested Hakes and Sauer, Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2006, show on-base percentage was underpaid until the book appeared, but how much of the A's 2002 success it explains is disputed.

Right or wrong?

Partly. The core idea holds: on-base percentage was underpaid and the A's exploited it, until the gap closed by 2004. The popular telling overstates it by skipping the players who were not bargain finds, including Zito, Tejada and Chavez.

Why this rating

Hakes and Sauer robustly show that on-base percentage was underpaid relative to its contribution to winning before 2004, and that the gap closed once the book appeared. The hot hand fallacy, and its reversal

“He's on fire, give him the ball.”

The hot hand fallacy, and its reversal. Believing in streaks was long the textbook error, but corrected analysis now finds a real, modest hot hand.

In full

For three decades the belief that a shooter on a streak is more likely to score next was the textbook example of seeing patterns in randomness. In 2018 Miller and Sanjurjo showed that the original test was biased against finding a streak effect. Corrected analyses of the same data find a real, modest hot hand. The fallacy that lasted longest may have been the claim of a fallacy.

Where it comes from

Gilovich, T., Vallone, R. and Tversky, A. (1985) 'The hot hand in basketball: On the misperception of random sequences', Cognitive Psychology, 17(3), 295-314, DOI 10.1016/0010-0285(85)90010-6. The paper used Philadelphia 76ers field goals, Boston Celtics free throws and a controlled experiment with Cornell varsity players. The reversal: Miller, J.B. and Sanjurjo, A. (2018) 'Surprised by the hot hand fallacy? A truth in the law of small numbers', Econometrica, 86(6), 2019-2047, DOI 10.3982/ECTA14943. Follow-up: Miller and Sanjurjo, 'A cold shower for the hot hand fallacy: Robust evidence from controlled settings', Review of Economics and Statistics, 106(6), 1607-1619 (2024).

The moment

One sales hire closes three enterprise deals in a quarter. The founder wants to hand them the whole pipeline and a team.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Big decisions based on one good quarter
  • Streaks dismissed as 'just variance' without checking
  • Nobody asking what conditions produced the results

Ask in the room

Will the territory, leads and deal size behind this streak still be there once they're managing?

In a founder-led company

A founder sees one sales hire close three enterprise deals in a quarter and wants to hand them the whole pipeline and a team. The bias lesson applies both ways. A short streak is weak evidence on its own, but rejecting it as 'just variance' is also a statistical choice. The useful question is whether the conditions behind the streak (territory, inbound leads, deal size) will still hold once the person is managing rather than selling.

What it costs

Treat a short streak as proof, or wave it away as luck, and you either promote on noise or ignore real form. Either way a big decision rests on thin evidence.

Try this week

Take one person or product on a streak. List the conditions behind it and mark which will still hold next quarter.

What to change

Use it for promotions, budget shifts after a strong quarter and channel bets. Judge the streak by the conditions that produced it, and check whether they will last.

Rating

Contested Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky, Cognitive Psychology, 1985, corrected by Miller and Sanjurjo, Econometrica, 2018. Controlled data now show a modest hot hand; in live games its size is still disputed.

Right or wrong?

The original 'fallacy' was itself wrong: a 2018 correction found a real, modest hot hand in the same kind of data. That doesn't make fans right, since people may still overrate streaks, and in live games the size of the effect is unsettled.

Why this rating

The selection bias is mathematically established and controlled shooting data now show a hot hand, but in live games shot difficulty and defensive adjustment obscure the effect, and researchers still disagree on its size.

Ringelmann effect and social loafing

“"Too many cooks" / "everyone assumed someone else had it"”

Ringelmann effect and social loafing. As a group gets bigger, each person tends to put in less, partly through poor coordination and partly because their own effort can't be seen.

In full

As a group gets bigger, the average effort or output per person tends to fall. Part of this is coordination loss, where people get in each other's way, and part is motivation loss, where individuals ease off when their own contribution cannot be seen. The motivation part is called social loafing and shrinks when individual work is identifiable, valued and evaluated.

Where it comes from

Max Ringelmann, agricultural engineer, ran rope-pulling and other tests at the Grand-Jouan agricultural school between 1882 and 1887 and published them as Ringelmann, M. (1913). Recherches sur les moteurs animés: Travail de l'homme. Annales de l'Institut National Agronomique, 2e série, tome 12, 1-40 (volume and page details vary between sources). Rediscovered in Kravitz, D. A. & Martin, B. (1986). Ringelmann rediscovered: The original article. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 50(5), 936-941. Ingham, A. G., Levinger, G., Graves, J. & Peckham, V. (1974). The Ringelmann effect: Studies of group size and group performance. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 10(4), 371-384. Latané, B., Williams, K. & Harkins, S. (1979). Many hands make light the work: The causes and consequences of social loafing. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 37(6), 822-832. Karau, S. J. & Williams, K. D. (1993). Social loafing: A meta-analytic review and theoretical integration. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 65(4), 681-706.

The moment

Three founder-led squads become one 'cross-functional' launch group of fifteen. Nobody owns the release notes, the pricing page or the customer email, and each person assumes one of the other fourteen has it.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • Tasks assigned to 'the team' with no named owner
  • Large meetings where nobody leaves with an action
  • Gaps found after launch that everyone assumed were covered

Ask in the room

For each item on this launch list, whose name is next to it?

In a founder-led company

A scaleup moves from three founder-led product squads to one 'cross-functional' launch group of fifteen; nobody owns the release notes, the pricing page or the customer email, and each person reasonably assumes one of the other fourteen will pick them up.

What it costs

Work that belongs to everyone ships late or not at all, and launches go out with gaps nobody noticed. Adding people can lower what each one delivers.

Try this week

Take your biggest cross-functional project and put one name against every deliverable. Anything that can't get a name gets cut or reassigned.

What to change

Use it whenever a group grows past a handful of people or work is assigned to a team rather than a person. Keep groups small, make each contribution visible and reviewed, and give every deliverable a single owner.

Rating

Robust Ringelmann, Annales de l'Institut National Agronomique, 1913, and Karau and Williams' meta-analysis of 78 studies, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1993. Social loafing is robust, and it shrinks when individual work is identifiable, valued and evaluated.

Right or wrong?

Proven. Per-person effort does fall as groups grow, and a meta-analysis of 78 studies found social loafing across tasks and populations. The scope limit: it shrinks when individual work is identifiable and valued, and Ringelmann himself put most of the loss down to coordination.

Why this rating

Karau and Williams' meta-analysis of 78 studies found social loafing across tasks and populations, with well-mapped moderators such as identifiability, task value and group size.

The Yerkes-Dodson law

“"A bit of pressure brings out the best in people"; the optimal stress curve”

The Yerkes-Dodson law. The popular idea that performance rises with stress up to a point and then falls, loosely drawn from a 1908 study of mice.

In full

The popular version says performance rises with arousal or stress up to a point and then falls, in an inverted U, with the peak coming earlier on harder tasks. The 1908 study behind it measured how quickly mice learned a visual discrimination under different strengths of electric shock. It said nothing about arousal, stress or human performance.

Where it comes from

Robert M. Yerkes and John D. Dodson, 'The relation of strength of stimulus to rapidity of habit-formation', Journal of Comparative Neurology and Psychology 18, 459-482, 1908 (dancing mice, black-white box discrimination, shock strength varied, task difficulty varied by brightness contrast). The 'law' label appears later (cited to Young 1936 and Eysenck 1955). The arousal reading followed Hebb's 1955 arousal concept and Broadhurst's 1957 and 1959 papers reviving the law. Critiques: Karl Halvor Teigen, 'Yerkes-Dodson: A law for all seasons', Theory & Psychology, 1994, doi:10.1177/0959354394044004; Martin Corbett, 'From law to folklore: work stress and the Yerkes-Dodson Law', Journal of Managerial Psychology 30(6), 741-752, 2015, doi:10.1108/JMP-03-2013-0085.

The moment

The founder justifies a permanent sprint culture with 'some stress is good for performance'. Six months later the leadership team is burning out.

Signs you’re looking at it

  • An inverted-U stress curve used to justify deadlines
  • Crunch treated as the normal way of working
  • 'Some stress is good for us' used to end discussion

Ask in the room

What evidence do we have that this level of pressure is improving our results?

In a founder-led company

A founder justifies a permanent sprint culture and impossible deadlines on the grounds that 'some stress is good for performance'. Six months later the leadership team is burning out and the founder is the first to go.

What it costs

Burnout among the people you can least afford to lose. In this case the founder was the first to go.

Try this week

Look at your last three crunch periods. Write down what each one delivered and what it cost in mistakes, sick days or departures.

What to change

Use it when pressure is defended by appeal to a stress curve. Set deadlines by what the work needs, and treat sustained crunch as a cost to manage.

Rating

Folk wisdom Yerkes and Dodson, Journal of Comparative Neurology and Psychology, 1908. The study measured how fast mice learned under electric shock; the human stress curve was added later and has never been established.

Right or wrong?

Wrong as popularly told. Yerkes and Dodson studied how fast mice learned under electric shock; they didn't study stress, arousal or humans, draw an inverted U or call it a law. Task difficulty did matter in their data, but the workplace stress curve has never been established.

Why this rating

The arousal-performance inverted U is a later reinterpretation of a small animal-learning study, and Teigen (1994) and Corbett (2015) show its variables were relabelled over decades while the human workplace version has never been established.
