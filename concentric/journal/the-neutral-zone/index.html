The neutral zone: why coaching and networking break down

Every woman we've spoken with this year had already tried something.

Every woman we've spoken with this year had already tried something. Coaching, usually. A networking group. A leadership programme sold as the fix for whatever wasn't working. Most of it helped a little, for a while. None of it addressed the real problem, because the real problem isn't a skills gap or a confidence gap. It's that these women are standing in a period the structures around them weren't built for.

William Bridges drew the distinction that matters here decades ago. Change is the external event: the job ends, the role shifts, the identity that organised your working life stops fitting. Transition is what happens internally, and it moves slower. Bridges called the middle of it the neutral zone, the stretch where the old identity has dissolved and the new one hasn't formed yet. Not a crisis. A period with no scaffolding.

Coaching needs a direction to coach toward. Networking needs a stable professional identity to perform. Leadership development assumes you already know, roughly, where you're headed. All three presuppose that the person walking in has settled who she's becoming. The neutral zone is defined by not having settled that yet. So the support most available to professional women is built for the phase before this one, or the phase after it, and not for the one they're actually in.

Concentric's research this year, drawn from 67 women surveyed through July, found the same mismatch stated four separate ways. The most common concern wasn't a stalled title or a difficult manager. It was direction: whether the current trajectory pointed anywhere worth going. One respondent put it plainly. “Working out whether I am on the right career path at all.” That's not a performance problem. It's a strategic question with nowhere to be asked out loud, since admitting it at work carries reputational risk in cultures where confidence and competence get read as the same trait.

The same pattern shows up under different names. Burnout, which most respondents described as recovery from an unsustainable role rather than a personal failure to manage stress, tracks with Gallup's finding that 59% of women report burnout against 46% of men, a gap that widens to twelve points among leaders specifically. Isolation, managed alone by nearly every respondent, matches Fortune and Infillion's finding that 80% of women experience professional loneliness, and that it intensifies with seniority rather than easing. And access: not a shortage of contacts but of sponsors willing to advocate, a gap McKinsey traces partly to affinity bias in who gets actively championed and who just gets a coffee.

What connects all four is the structural gap Bridges named. Existing support treats the symptoms, thin network, low visibility, burnout, as the problem itself, when each is downstream of a period that has no design specification anywhere in professional life. You cannot coach someone toward a direction she's still questioning. You cannot network into sponsorship if the rooms reward people who already look settled. The women managing this alone aren't doing it by temperament. Disclosure has a cost that silence doesn't, so most choose silence.

None of this makes coaching or networking bad tools. They're the right tools for a different phase, the one after direction has re-formed, not the one during which it hasn't. In Concentric's research, Career Reinvention and Career Clarity together accounted for more than half of all identified needs, more than leadership challenges, more than visibility, more than anything else named. Not a plan someone can hand over. Something worked out only in the company of people doing the same thing, at the same time.
